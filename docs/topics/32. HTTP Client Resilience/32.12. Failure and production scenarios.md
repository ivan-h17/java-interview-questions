# Failure and production scenarios

---

<a id="hcr-scen-001"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>HCR-SCEN-001. A downstream service becomes slow and your service exhausts request threads. What do you change?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Add or tighten deadlines, isolate the dependency, reduce concurrency, stop unsafe retries and degrade or fail fast.

#### Interview answer

First I would stop the bleeding: reduce or disable retries, lower excessive timeouts, open or respect the circuit breaker, shed optional traffic and apply a concurrency limit for that dependency. Then I would isolate the dependency with a separate pool/bulkhead so unrelated endpoints keep working.

I would verify thread dumps, pool metrics, timeout rates and traces to confirm where threads are blocked.

#### Deep dive

If the dependency is optional, return degraded responses. If it is critical, fail clearly rather than letting requests hang.

#### Production angle

The goal is to protect local resources while the dependency recovers or the owner fixes it.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not just increase thread count.
- Do not increase timeout during saturation without checking impact.
- Retries can make the slow dependency slower.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Best immediate protection from slow downstream thread exhaustion?
A: Timeouts, bulkheads and reduced retry/concurrency.

</details>

<a id="hcr-scen-002"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>HCR-SCEN-002. Retries make an outage worse. What went wrong?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Retries likely amplified load, ignored deadlines, retried unsafe failures or happened at multiple layers.

#### Interview answer

I would check original calls versus retry attempts, retry success rate, downstream QPS, timeout rate and pool saturation. Common causes are immediate retries without backoff/jitter, retries at gateway/service/SDK layers, retrying non-retryable errors, no retry budget or retries continuing after the caller deadline.

The fix is to throttle or disable retries, add backoff and jitter, respect `Retry-After`, enforce deadlines and coordinate retry ownership.

#### Deep dive

Retries are useful only when failures are transient and capacity remains. During overload, they often make recovery harder.

#### Production angle

Retry policies should be controlled by config and observable separately from original demand.

#### Traps / caveats

- Retry success without latency budget is not success.
- SDKs may retry invisibly.
- Retrying writes can duplicate side effects.

#### Optional Anki

Q: First metric to inspect in retry storm?
A: Original calls versus retry attempts.

</details>

<a id="hcr-scen-003"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>HCR-SCEN-003. A dashboard endpoint depends on inventory, pricing and recommendations, and recommendations start timing out. What response and telemetry would you design?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Keep required inventory/pricing if healthy, omit or degrade recommendations explicitly and emit fallback metrics.

#### Interview answer

I would classify recommendations as optional unless the product requires them. The endpoint should use a short timeout for recommendations and return the dashboard with a clear missing/degraded recommendations field. Inventory and pricing should remain correct and should not wait behind recommendation failures.

Telemetry should include recommendation timeout count, fallback/degraded response count, latency impact and traces showing the failed optional dependency.

#### Deep dive

If clients need to render differently, the API contract should expose degraded state rather than silently returning an empty list that looks normal.

#### Production angle

This keeps the main user flow available while making the recommendation outage visible.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not let optional calls consume the whole deadline.
- Do not degrade pricing/inventory casually.
- Alert on sustained degraded responses.

#### Optional Anki

Q: How treat failed optional recommendations?
A: Short timeout, explicit degradation, telemetry.

</details>

<a id="hcr-scen-004"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>HCR-SCEN-004. A third-party API starts returning `429`. What should your service do immediately and long term?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Immediately reduce traffic and respect retry guidance; long term enforce quota-aware throttling and prioritization.

#### Interview answer

Immediately, I would stop aggressive retries, respect `Retry-After`, reduce concurrency, shed optional/batch traffic and protect user-critical paths. I would check whether a deploy, batch job or tenant caused the spike.

Long term, add client-side throttling, quota dashboards, tenant/job prioritization, alerting before quota exhaustion and possibly caching, batching or higher quota.

#### Deep dive

If the quota is fleet-wide, per-instance limits must account for replica count and autoscaling.

#### Production angle

Quota incidents are dependency incidents and capacity-planning incidents at the same time.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not retry `429` immediately.
- Do not let background jobs starve user traffic.
- Do not assume adding replicas helps; it may increase quota consumption.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What should happen first after sustained `429`?
A: Reduce outgoing rate and stop harmful retries.

</details>

<a id="hcr-scen-005"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>HCR-SCEN-005. A downstream returns `503` for 5% of calls. How do you decide whether to retry?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Check operation safety, deadline, retry success probability, downstream load and whether `Retry-After` is provided.

#### Interview answer

For safe reads or idempotent operations, a small bounded retry may improve success if failures are transient and latency budget allows it. For non-idempotent writes, retry only with idempotency protection.

I would measure whether retries actually succeed, whether p99 latency remains acceptable and whether retry traffic increases downstream overload.

#### Deep dive

`503` may come from the downstream, gateway, mesh or load balancer. The source matters for policy.

#### Production angle

If retries do not improve final success or increase saturation, reduce them and consider circuit breaking or fail-fast behavior.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not retry every `503` forever.
- Respect `Retry-After` if present.
- Retry different instances only when safe.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Key retry decision for `503`?
A: Safe operation plus useful success within deadline.

</details>

<a id="hcr-scen-006"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>HCR-SCEN-006. Latency spikes after every deployment, then disappears. What client-side causes would you inspect?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Inspect cold pools, DNS, TLS handshakes, warmup, connection reuse, credentials and rollout traffic shape.

#### Interview answer

After deployment, new pods may have empty connection pools, cold TLS sessions, DNS lookup cost, JIT/classloading warmup or missing warmed caches. If traffic shifts quickly, many pods may create connections at once and overload the downstream or load balancer.

I would compare latency phases: pool acquisition, connect/TLS, first request versus later requests, and dependency-side connection metrics.

#### Deep dive

Canaries may hide this if they receive too little traffic to expose cold-start behavior before full rollout.

#### Production angle

Mitigations include gradual rollout, connection warmup where safe, sane timeouts and downstream connection limits.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not assume app code got slower.
- TLS handshake may exceed very tight timeouts.
- Warmup must not create unsafe side effects.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Common post-deploy HTTP latency cause?
A: Cold connections and TLS handshakes.

</details>

<a id="hcr-scen-007"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>HCR-SCEN-007. The HTTP client connection pool is full, but downstream latency looks normal. What do you check?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Check unclosed responses, pool acquisition waits, local concurrency, long body reads, queueing and per-route pool limits.

#### Interview answer

If downstream server latency looks fine, the issue may be local. I would check whether response bodies are fully consumed/closed, whether large or streaming responses hold connections, whether per-route limits are too low, whether callers queue too many concurrent requests and whether retries are increasing in-flight calls.

I would also inspect pool metrics by route and thread dumps.

#### Deep dive

Server-side latency usually measures time after the request arrives. It may not include local pool wait before the request is sent.

#### Production angle

Pool saturation can be a client leak or configuration issue, not necessarily a downstream outage.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not fix blindly by increasing pool size.
- Check error paths for response cleanup.
- Separate pool wait from downstream latency in metrics.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Pool full but downstream normal means check what first?
A: Client-side leaks, pool waits and concurrency.

</details>

<a id="hcr-scen-008"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>HCR-SCEN-008. A payment creation call times out after the request was sent. How do you recover safely?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Treat the outcome as unknown, retry only with the same idempotency key or check operation status, then reconcile.

#### Interview answer

I would not assume the payment failed. The server or provider may have created it. The client should use the same idempotency key on retry, query payment status if available and avoid creating a second payment command.

If the status remains unknown, record it for reconciliation and return a state like "processing" or "unknown, check later" rather than charging again.

#### Deep dive

The idempotency record should be durable and tied to the business transaction. External provider idempotency should be coordinated with internal idempotency.

#### Production angle

Payment timeout handling should be designed and tested before incidents; manual cleanup is expensive and risky.

#### Traps / caveats

- Timeout does not mean failed.
- Do not retry with a new key.
- Reconciliation jobs are required for ambiguous states.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Safe interpretation of timed-out payment creation?
A: Unknown outcome.

</details>

<a id="hcr-scen-009"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>HCR-SCEN-009. One dependency is failing and unrelated endpoints are slowing down. How do you limit blast radius?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Isolate the dependency with bulkheads, pool limits, deadlines, circuit breakers and feature disablement.

#### Interview answer

Unrelated endpoints slow down when they share threads, executors, HTTP pools, CPU, memory or retry budgets with the failing dependency. I would identify the shared resource and isolate it: separate pool, smaller concurrency limit, shorter timeout, open circuit breaker, disable optional calls and shed low-priority work.

I would confirm with thread dumps, pool metrics and traces.

#### Deep dive

Blast radius is not only code dependency. Shared infrastructure like NAT gateways, proxies or rate limits can also spread failure.

#### Production angle

Good service design reserves capacity for critical paths and makes optional dependencies unable to starve them.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not let optional work share all critical resources.
- Increasing global thread count may hide the issue briefly.
- Fallback must not overload another dependency.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Pattern for limiting dependency blast radius?
A: Bulkhead.

</details>

<a id="hcr-scen-010"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>HCR-SCEN-010. A circuit breaker is open during an incident. What should you inspect before changing thresholds?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Check whether the dependency is healthy, failures are classified correctly, retry pressure exists and half-open probes are safe.

#### Interview answer

Before changing thresholds, I would inspect dependency health, recent errors, timeout rates, slow calls, breaker metrics, retry volume, traffic level and fallback impact. I would verify that the breaker counted real dependency failures rather than caller validation errors.

Closing or loosening a breaker can flood a recovering dependency, so recovery should be gradual.

#### Deep dive

Half-open settings matter. Too many probes can overload recovery; too few can delay return to service.

#### Production angle

Breaker changes during incidents should be deliberate, logged and monitored.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not close a breaker just because a status page is green.
- Do not count business errors as dependency failures.
- Watch retry traffic before reopening.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Why be careful closing a circuit breaker?
A: It can send full traffic to a dependency not ready for it.

</details>

<a id="hcr-scen-011"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>HCR-SCEN-011. Outbound calls fail only under high concurrency. What are likely bottlenecks?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Likely bottlenecks include client pools, per-route limits, executor saturation, downstream limits, rate limits, NAT/ports, CPU and response cleanup.

#### Interview answer

I would inspect HTTP pool active/pending counts, connection acquisition timeouts, executor queues, request thread counts, downstream `429`/`503`, per-route connection limits, large body handling, TLS handshakes, DNS behavior and whether responses are leaked.

At infrastructure level, check NAT gateway limits, ephemeral ports, service mesh limits and load balancer connection limits.

#### Deep dive

High-concurrency failures often expose local limits that low traffic never reaches.

#### Production angle

Concurrency tests should run before increasing traffic or migrating to virtual threads.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not assume the downstream is slow.
- Increasing concurrency can reduce throughput.
- Check both application and network infrastructure limits.

#### Optional Anki

Q: First local metric for high-concurrency outbound failures?
A: HTTP pool pending/acquisition timeouts.

</details>

<a id="hcr-scen-012"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>🧠 HCR-SCEN-012. How would you safely roll out a new resilience policy for a critical downstream dependency?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Roll out gradually with feature flags/config, strong metrics, canaries, rollback and clear success criteria.

#### Interview answer

For a critical dependency, I would avoid a big-bang change. First, add metrics to observe current behavior. Then deploy the new policy disabled or in shadow/metrics-only mode if possible. Enable for a small percentage, one instance, one tenant or one operation. Watch success rate, p99 latency, retries, pool waits, breaker state and business outcomes.

Rollback should be a config change or feature flag, not a new build.

#### Deep dive

For risky changes, run load tests and failure injection before production. Coordinate with the dependency owner if the policy changes traffic patterns.

#### Production angle

A bad resilience policy can cause the exact incident it was meant to prevent.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not change timeout, retry and breaker thresholds all at once without observability.
- Do not roll out during an active unrelated incident.
- Include business metrics, not only technical metrics.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Safe rollout tool for resilience policy?
A: Canary or feature-flagged configuration with rollback.

</details>
