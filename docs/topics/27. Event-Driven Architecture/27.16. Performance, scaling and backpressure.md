# Performance, scaling and backpressure

---

<a id="eda-scale-001"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>EDA-SCALE-001. How do throughput, latency and correctness trade off in event-driven systems?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Higher throughput often uses batching and parallelism, which can increase latency and complicate ordering or correctness.

#### Interview answer

Batching improves producer and consumer throughput but waits to collect records. More consumer concurrency drains backlog faster but can break per-key ordering or overload downstream databases. Correctness constraints like idempotency, transactions, and strict ordering may limit raw speed.

#### Deep dive

Performance design starts with business requirements: maximum delay, required ordering, acceptable duplicates, and recovery behavior. Then choose partitioning, batching, concurrency, and storage strategy.

#### Production angle

Measure end-to-end event age, not only broker throughput. A system can publish fast and still be slow to produce business effects.

#### Traps / caveats

- Scaling consumers can move the bottleneck to the database.
- Low latency and high throughput often need different settings.
- Correctness shortcuts create expensive incidents.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What can batching trade for throughput?
A: Higher latency.

</details>

<a id="eda-scale-002"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>EDA-SCALE-002. What is backpressure in an event-driven architecture?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Backpressure is a signal or mechanism that prevents producers or consumers from overwhelming downstream capacity.

#### Interview answer

If consumers cannot keep up, queues grow, lag increases, and downstream dependencies may overload. Backpressure can mean slowing producers, pausing consumers, limiting concurrency, applying rate limits, shedding optional work, or buffering with clear limits.

#### Deep dive

Asynchronous systems can hide overload temporarily by buffering. That is useful for spikes, but dangerous if producers continuously exceed processing capacity.

#### Production angle

Use lag and oldest age metrics to trigger scaling or throttling before storage fills or SLAs are missed.

#### Traps / caveats

- A queue is not infinite capacity.
- Autoscaling consumers can overload databases.
- Dropping events is only safe for explicitly lossy use cases.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What does backpressure prevent?
A: Producers or consumers overwhelming downstream capacity.

</details>

<a id="eda-scale-003"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>EDA-SCALE-003. How do you scale consumers safely?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Increase parallelism within ordering limits, protect downstream dependencies, and keep processing idempotent.

#### Interview answer

Scaling consumers may mean more instances, more partitions, higher concurrency, or larger batches. Before scaling, check bottlenecks: broker partitions, database connection pool, external API limits, locks, and hot keys.

#### Deep dive

Safe scaling requires consumer processing to be stateless or partition-aware. Per-key ordering must be preserved when required. Acknowledgement and transaction boundaries must remain correct under concurrency.

#### Production angle

Scale based on lag, processing latency, CPU, downstream saturation, and error rate, not CPU alone.

#### Traps / caveats

- More consumers than partitions may not help.
- More concurrency can increase duplicates during failures.
- Downstream rate limits can make scaling harmful.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What must be checked before adding consumers?
A: Ordering, partitions, idempotency, and downstream capacity.

</details>

<a id="eda-scale-004"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>EDA-SCALE-004. When are batching and small event payloads useful?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Batching improves throughput, while small payloads reduce broker, network, storage, and consumer cost.

#### Interview answer

Producers can batch records to reduce network round trips. Consumers can batch database writes or acknowledgements. Keeping payloads small avoids large memory spikes, slow serialization, expensive retention, and broker limits.

#### Deep dive

Batching must respect failure behavior. If one record in a batch fails, the system needs a policy for partial success, retry, split, or DLQ.

#### Production angle

Large event payloads often indicate that binary files or huge documents should be stored elsewhere, with the event carrying a reference and metadata.

#### Traps / caveats

- Batching can increase latency.
- Batch retries can duplicate already-processed records.
- Too-small events can force costly callback lookups.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Why avoid huge event payloads?
A: They increase broker, network, memory, storage, and replay costs.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>🧠 EDA-SCALE-005. How do fan-out, hot partitions and load shedding affect capacity planning?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Fan-out multiplies work, hot partitions limit parallelism, and load shedding protects critical capacity during overload.

#### Interview answer

One event may trigger ten consumers and many downstream writes. A hot key can keep one partition overloaded while others are idle. Load shedding may drop or defer non-critical work so critical flows continue.

#### Deep dive

Capacity planning should model event amplification: producer rate multiplied by consumer count, handler cost, downstream calls, retries, and replay/backfill load.

#### Production angle

Separate critical and non-critical streams where possible. Use quotas, priority, throttling, and isolated consumer pools to prevent one flow from starving another.

#### Traps / caveats

- Adding consumers increases platform cost and blast radius.
- Hot partitions are not fixed by adding generic consumers.
- Load shedding must not drop required business facts.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What is event amplification?
A: One event causing many downstream handlers and side effects.

</details>
