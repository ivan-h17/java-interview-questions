# Senior scenario-based event-driven design questions

---

<a id="eda-scen-001"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>EDA-SCEN-001. How would you design an `OrderCreated` event flow for payment, inventory, notification and analytics?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Commit the order, publish `OrderCreated` reliably via Outbox, and let independent consumers handle payment, inventory, notification, and analytics with idempotency and monitoring.

#### Interview answer

The order service owns order state and emits `OrderCreated` after commit. Payment and inventory either participate in a Saga or are orchestrated by a process manager. Notification and analytics consume independently. Every consumer uses stable event IDs, idempotent processing, retries, DLQ, and correlation IDs.

#### Deep dive

Payment and inventory are business-critical and need explicit workflow state such as `PENDING_PAYMENT`, `RESERVED`, `CONFIRMED`, or `FAILED`. Notification and analytics can usually lag and should not block checkout.

#### Production angle

Monitor outbox lag, broker lag, consumer failures, stuck orders by state, duplicate side effects, and DLQ depth.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not make notification failure roll back order creation.
- Do not charge payment twice on retry.
- Do not assume all consumers finish immediately.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What should protect `OrderCreated` publication?
A: Transactional Outbox.

</details>

<a id="eda-scen-002"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>EDA-SCEN-002. How would you design payment and inventory reservation as a Saga?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Use local transactions for each step, explicit Saga state, idempotent commands, timeouts, and compensation.

#### Interview answer

The order starts as pending. The Saga requests payment authorization and inventory reservation. If both succeed, the order is confirmed. If one fails, the Saga releases the other resource or cancels the order. Each participant records idempotency keys so duplicate commands do not repeat effects.

#### Deep dive

Orchestration is often clearer here because payment and inventory outcomes must be coordinated. Timeouts should move the Saga to retry, compensate, or manual review depending on business rules.

#### Production angle

Expose order status to users and alert on Sagas stuck in pending or compensating states.

#### Traps / caveats

- Timeout does not prove the remote step failed.
- Compensation can fail.
- Inventory and payment should not share a database just to simplify the workflow.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What protects Saga steps from duplicate commands?
A: Idempotency keys and durable step state.

</details>

<a id="eda-scen-003"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>EDA-SCEN-003. A consumer falls several hours behind. How do you diagnose and recover?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Check whether the bottleneck is producer spike, broker issue, consumer error, downstream dependency, hot partition, or deployment regression.

#### Interview answer

I would inspect lag by partition/key, processing latency, error rate, retries, DLQ, recent deployments, downstream database/API metrics, consumer concurrency, and broker health. Recovery may involve rollback, fixing poison messages, scaling consumers, pausing non-critical work, or throttling producers.

#### Deep dive

If one partition is lagging, adding consumers may not help. If the database is saturated, adding consumers can make it worse. If retries are blocking processing, move bad messages to quarantine after audit.

#### Production angle

Track oldest message age and business SLA impact. Communicate whether side effects are delayed, lost, or safely backlogged.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not blindly scale consumers.
- Do not reset offsets without understanding data loss.
- Catch-up can overload downstream systems.

#### Optional Anki

Q: First lag diagnosis split?
A: Producer volume, broker health, consumer errors, downstream bottleneck, or hot partition.

</details>

<a id="eda-scen-004"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>EDA-SCEN-004. A producer published a bad event or breaking schema change to production. What are your recovery options?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Stop or roll back the producer, protect consumers, quarantine bad events, publish corrected events if needed, and fix compatibility long term.

#### Interview answer

Immediately assess affected consumers and business impact. Roll back or disable the producer if it is still publishing bad events. Pause vulnerable consumers or route bad messages to DLQ. Then decide whether to replay, publish corrective events, backfill, or repair projections.

#### Deep dive

For schema breaks, restore compatibility by accepting both old and new formats or republishing a compatible version. For wrong business facts, do not silently edit history; use correction events or documented repair.

#### Production angle

After recovery, add schema checks, contract tests, sample payload validation, and deployment gates.

#### Traps / caveats

- Deleting events from a log is rarely a complete fix.
- Replaying bad events can repeat the incident.
- Some consumers may be unknown without a catalog.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Immediate action for bad event stream?
A: Stop further damage, protect consumers, then repair or correct state.

</details>

<a id="eda-scen-005"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>EDA-SCEN-005. A DLQ starts filling after a deployment. What do you inspect and roll back?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Compare deployment changes against DLQ errors, payloads, schema versions, consumer logs, and downstream health.

#### Interview answer

I would inspect failure reason distribution, first failure time, consumer version, producer version, sample DLQ payloads, deserialization errors, schema registry changes, downstream dependency errors, and recent configuration changes. If the new version is clearly responsible, roll it back or disable the consumer.

#### Deep dive

Not every DLQ spike is a consumer bug. It can be bad producer data, missing schema compatibility, dependency outage, changed credentials, or a poison event.

#### Production angle

Stop the bleed first, preserve failed messages, then redrive only after the fix is verified.

#### Traps / caveats

- Redriving before fixing root cause loops failures.
- Rolling back consumers may not help if producer schema changed.
- DLQ payloads may contain sensitive data.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What should you do before DLQ redrive?
A: Identify and fix the failure cause.

</details>

<a id="eda-scen-006"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>EDA-SCEN-006. A payment is charged twice after a retry. What design gaps do you look for?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Look for missing idempotency keys, non-transactional state, unsafe retry policy, and ambiguous timeout handling.

#### Interview answer

I would check whether the payment request used a stable provider idempotency key, whether local payment operation state was persisted, whether duplicate events or commands were deduplicated, and whether timeout recovery queried payment status before retrying.

#### Deep dive

Charging twice usually means the system treated "no response" as "not charged". In distributed systems, timeout means unknown. The design must handle unknown outcomes with idempotency and reconciliation.

#### Production angle

Immediate response includes stopping duplicate retries, identifying affected customers, reconciling with payment provider records, and issuing refunds or reversals.

#### Traps / caveats

- Event ID alone may not be the provider idempotency key.
- Retrying a payment call without provider support is dangerous.
- Database transaction rollback does not roll back external payment.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What does timeout mean for payment?
A: Unknown outcome, not guaranteed failure.

</details>

<a id="eda-scen-007"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>EDA-SCEN-007. The broker is unavailable during checkout. How should the service behave?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

If checkout state can commit locally, write to Outbox and return a clear order state; if durable event recording is impossible, fail safely.

#### Interview answer

With Transactional Outbox, checkout does not need the broker to be available at request time. The service commits order state plus outbox row, and the relay publishes later. If the local database is available but broker is down, side effects are delayed. If no durable event record can be written, the service should not pretend downstream processing will happen.

#### Deep dive

The user-facing response depends on business rules. It may return `PENDING_CONFIRMATION` if payment/inventory are asynchronous, or fail checkout if immediate confirmation is required.

#### Production angle

Alert on outbox lag and broker outage. After recovery, drain outbox carefully and monitor downstream backlog.

#### Traps / caveats

- Direct broker publish in checkout creates availability coupling.
- Returning success without durable intent can lose work.
- Draining backlog can overload consumers after broker recovery.

#### Optional Anki

Q: How does Outbox help when broker is down?
A: It durably records events in the local database for later publishing.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>🧠 EDA-SCEN-008. How would you design a multi-region event flow with clear ordering, privacy and failure trade-offs?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Define the consistency scope first, then choose regional ownership, replication, ordering keys, conflict handling, and privacy controls explicitly.

#### Interview answer

I would avoid promising global ordering unless the business requires it. Prefer regional ownership for writes, per-entity ordering within a region, replicated events for read models, and explicit conflict resolution for multi-writer cases. Sensitive data should be minimized or encrypted before cross-region replication.

#### Deep dive

Multi-region EDA has trade-offs between latency, availability, ordering, and consistency. Active-active designs need idempotency, conflict detection, causality metadata, and clear recovery behavior after partitions.

#### Production angle

Document RPO/RTO, failover behavior, replay windows, data residency constraints, and whether consumers may process duplicate or delayed cross-region events.

#### Traps / caveats

- Global ordering across regions is expensive and availability-sensitive.
- Data residency can forbid replicating full payloads.
- Failover can duplicate events or change ordering.

#### Optional Anki

Q: First question in multi-region event design?
A: What consistency and ordering scope is actually required?

</details>
