# Anti-patterns and common traps

---

<a id="eda-anti-001"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>EDA-ANTI-001. Why is treating events as RPC commands a common anti-pattern?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

It hides synchronous command coupling behind asynchronous infrastructure.

#### Interview answer

If a producer publishes an "event" expecting exactly one consumer to perform a required action immediately, it is probably a command or request. That creates unclear ownership, timeout ambiguity, and difficult error handling.

#### Deep dive

Events should describe facts. Commands should have clear receivers and intent. Mixing them makes systems hard to reason about because nobody knows who is responsible for completion.

#### Production angle

Use commands, workflow orchestration, or synchronous APIs when the producer needs a specific action before the business flow can continue.

#### Traps / caveats

- Request-reply over a broker is valid but should be explicit.
- Events can trigger workflows, but responsibility must be modeled.
- Fake events often lead to stuck processes and unclear retries.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Why avoid RPC disguised as events?
A: It creates hidden coupling and unclear responsibility.

</details>

<a id="eda-anti-002"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>EDA-ANTI-002. What is wrong with vague event names such as `DataChanged`?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

They do not communicate business meaning.

#### Interview answer

`DataChanged` forces consumers to inspect payloads or producer internals to know what happened. `OrderCancelled`, `PaymentCaptured`, or `CustomerEmailChanged` are clearer because they describe specific facts.

#### Deep dive

Specific names improve routing, schema ownership, observability, replay, and audit. Vague events often become generic database-change notifications and create tight coupling.

#### Production angle

During incidents, precise event names make it easier to understand what changed and which consumers should react.

#### Traps / caveats

- Overly granular events can also create noise.
- Technical change events may be valid in data pipelines but should be labeled as such.
- Names must match actual semantics.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Better alternative to `DataChanged`?
A: A specific business fact like `OrderCancelled`.

</details>

<a id="eda-anti-003"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>EDA-ANTI-003. What are the risks of putting too much or too little data into events?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Too much data leaks internals and increases cost; too little data creates chatty callbacks and coupling.

#### Interview answer

Large payloads increase serialization, retention, network, and privacy risk. Tiny ID-only events may force every consumer to call the producer, causing runtime coupling and thundering herds after replay.

#### Deep dive

Payload design should match consumer needs, privacy constraints, schema evolution, and replay behavior. Often the best event carries stable business identifiers, relevant state, and metadata, not entire database rows.

#### Production angle

Review event size, PII, consumer callback behavior, and retention cost before publishing a new event type.

#### Traps / caveats

- Full snapshots can leak private fields.
- ID-only events can overload producers during backfill.
- Payload choices are hard to change after many consumers depend on them.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Payload design trade-off?
A: Enough data for useful processing, not so much that it leaks internals or becomes costly.

</details>

<a id="eda-anti-004"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>EDA-ANTI-004. Why are shared databases, hidden synchronous calls and unbounded retries dangerous in event-driven systems?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

They reintroduce tight coupling, cascading failures, and overload behind an asynchronous facade.

#### Interview answer

If services share a database, events may only duplicate already-coupled state. If handlers secretly call producers synchronously, consumer failures depend on producer availability. If retries are unbounded, one outage can create a retry storm and large backlog.

#### Deep dive

These patterns make systems look decoupled while preserving old failure modes. Real EDA needs clear ownership, bounded dependencies, idempotency, backoff, and operational controls.

#### Production angle

Inspect event handlers for synchronous calls, database ownership violations, retry loops, and missing circuit breakers.

#### Traps / caveats

- Shared database may be acceptable only as a temporary migration compromise.
- Hidden synchronous calls hurt replay and recovery.
- Unbounded retries can block ordered streams.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Why are hidden synchronous calls inside handlers risky?
A: They recreate runtime coupling and cascading failure paths.

</details>

<a id="eda-anti-005"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>🧠 EDA-ANTI-005. How can cyclic event dependencies or Event Sourcing everywhere destabilize a system?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Cycles can cause feedback loops, and Event Sourcing everywhere adds unnecessary complexity and operational load.

#### Interview answer

A cycle appears when service A reacts to B and B reacts back to A indefinitely or through fragile state transitions. Event Sourcing everywhere forces every entity into event history, projections, upcasting, and replay even when simple current-state storage is enough.

#### Deep dive

Cycles need explicit workflow ownership and termination conditions. Event Sourcing should be chosen for domains that benefit from full historical state, not as a blanket architectural rule.

#### Production angle

Monitor event amplification, repeated event chains, and unexpected publish loops. Review whether event-sourced aggregates are actually using history.

#### Traps / caveats

- Feedback loops can be triggered by replay.
- Event Sourcing can complicate privacy deletion.
- Too many projections increase operational burden.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Why avoid Event Sourcing as a default?
A: It adds event history, projection, replay, and versioning complexity where current state may be enough.

</details>
