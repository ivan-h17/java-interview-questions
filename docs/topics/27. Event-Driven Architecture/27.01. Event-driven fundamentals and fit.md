# Event-driven fundamentals and fit

---

<a id="eda-fund-001"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>EDA-FUND-001. What is event-driven architecture?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Event-driven architecture is a style where services communicate by publishing and reacting to events.

#### Interview answer

An event represents something that already happened, such as `OrderCreated` or `PaymentCaptured`. Producers publish events without directly controlling every consumer. Consumers subscribe and perform their own work, such as updating a read model, sending a notification, or starting another business step.

#### Deep dive

The important shift is from direct command flow to state-change notification. A service still owns its data and business rules, but it exposes important facts through an event contract. This can reduce temporal coupling because producer and consumer do not need to be available at the same instant.

#### Production angle

EDA is useful for workflows, integration between services, audit-like streams, read-model updates, analytics feeds, and decoupling slow side effects from a request path.

#### Traps / caveats

- Events are not free: they add broker, schema, replay, monitoring, and failure-handling complexity.
- Asynchronous does not mean eventually correct by default.
- Event-driven design should not hide unclear ownership boundaries.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What does an event represent?
A: A fact that already happened in the system.

</details>

<a id="eda-fund-002"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>EDA-FUND-002. What backend problems does event-driven architecture solve, and what does it not solve?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

It helps decouple producers from consumers, absorb bursts, and model asynchronous workflows. It does not automatically solve consistency, ownership, or reliability problems.

#### Interview answer

EDA is good when several independent reactions should happen after a state change, or when slow downstream work should not block the main request. For example, after order creation, payment, inventory, notification, and analytics can react separately. But EDA does not remove the need for idempotency, transactions, schema governance, observability, and recovery.

#### Deep dive

It solves some coupling problems by replacing direct calls with a durable communication channel. It does not solve business ambiguity. If nobody owns the event contract, or the event contains unstable internal data, consumers still become tightly coupled.

#### Production angle

Use events where delayed processing is acceptable and recovery matters. Avoid them for simple CRUD where synchronous confirmation and immediate consistency are required.

#### Traps / caveats

- Messaging can make debugging harder.
- Eventual consistency must be visible in product behavior.
- A broker does not fix bad service boundaries.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What does EDA not solve automatically?
A: Consistency, idempotency, ownership, observability, and schema evolution.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>EDA-FUND-003. How is event-driven architecture different from synchronous request-response design?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Synchronous request-response expects an immediate answer from a known receiver. EDA publishes facts for interested consumers to process asynchronously.

#### Interview answer

In synchronous HTTP or gRPC, service A calls service B and waits for success, failure, or timeout. In EDA, service A publishes an event after something happens, and consumers react independently. The producer usually does not know all consumers and does not wait for their work to finish.

#### Deep dive

Synchronous communication is easier to reason about for immediate reads, validation, and user-facing decisions. EDA is better for fan-out, buffering, workflow continuation, and independent downstream processing. The trade-off is that state may be temporarily inconsistent.

#### Production angle

Checkout may synchronously validate cart and payment authorization, but asynchronously send notifications, update analytics, and refresh search indexes.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not use events when the caller needs an immediate authoritative answer.
- Do not simulate RPC over events without a strong reason.
- Timeouts move from the call stack to workflow state and monitoring.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Main difference between synchronous call and event publication?
A: A synchronous call waits for a known receiver; an event publishes a fact for asynchronous consumers.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>EDA-FUND-004. When is a synchronous API call a better choice than publishing an event?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Use a synchronous call when the caller needs an immediate decision, validation result, or strongly consistent response.

#### Interview answer

If a service cannot continue without an answer, a synchronous call is often clearer. Examples include checking authorization, loading data for a response, validating an invariant owned by another service, or calling a payment provider where the user expects an immediate outcome.

#### Deep dive

Events are good for "something happened" notification, not for every decision. If the producer needs a yes/no answer before committing, publishing an event and waiting for a reply usually recreates RPC with more complexity.

#### Production angle

Use synchronous APIs for request-path decisions with timeout budgets. Use events for after-commit side effects, fan-out, and background workflows where delayed completion is acceptable.

#### Traps / caveats

- Synchronous calls need timeouts, retries, and circuit breakers.
- Events can still be used after the synchronous decision is committed.
- Immediate consistency requirements should be explicit, not guessed.

#### Optional Anki

Q: When is HTTP/gRPC better than an event?
A: When an immediate authoritative response is needed.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>EDA-FUND-005. What coupling does asynchronous messaging reduce, and what coupling remains?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

It reduces temporal and direct runtime coupling, but schema, semantic, operational, and business-process coupling remain.

#### Interview answer

The producer does not need every consumer to be online, and it does not call each consumer directly. That reduces temporal coupling and fan-out complexity. But consumers still depend on the meaning, schema, ordering assumptions, and retention of the event stream.

#### Deep dive

EDA can hide coupling if teams treat events as public contracts without governance. A field rename, changed meaning, new required value, or changed ordering assumption can break consumers even though no synchronous API was called.

#### Production angle

Good event contracts need owners, compatibility tests, schema review, versioning, deprecation policy, and monitoring for lag and failures.

#### Traps / caveats

- "Loose coupling" does not mean "no contract".
- Consumers can become coupled to producer internals.
- Operational coupling appears when one event fan-outs to many expensive handlers.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What coupling remains in EDA?
A: Schema, semantic, ordering, operational, and business-process coupling.

</details>

<a id="eda-fund-006"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>🧠 EDA-FUND-006. How do you decide whether event-driven architecture is justified without creating a distributed monolith?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Use EDA when async workflows, fan-out, resilience, or independent consumers justify the complexity. Avoid it when services still behave like one tightly coupled call chain.

#### Interview answer

I would ask whether delayed consistency is acceptable, whether multiple independent consumers need the same fact, whether durable buffering matters, and whether teams can operate the broker, contracts, retries, and monitoring. If every step must happen in strict order and failure of one consumer blocks the whole business operation, a synchronous design or a single transaction boundary may be better.

#### Deep dive

A distributed monolith appears when services are split physically but remain tightly coupled semantically: shared database, chatty event chains, cyclic dependencies, hidden synchronous calls inside handlers, and coordinated deployments for every change.

#### Production angle

Look for independent deployability, clear ownership, bounded event contracts, observable workflow state, and safe replay. If these are missing, EDA can increase incident frequency instead of reducing coupling.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not introduce EDA only because "microservices need events".
- Do not split a simple transaction into many services without business need.
- Team maturity matters as much as technical fit.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What is a warning sign of event-driven distributed monolith?
A: Services communicate asynchronously but still require lockstep changes and strict chained execution.

</details>
