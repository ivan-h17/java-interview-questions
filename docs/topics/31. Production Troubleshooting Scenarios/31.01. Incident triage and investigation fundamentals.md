# Incident triage and investigation fundamentals

---

<a id="prod-fund-001"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>PROD-FUND-001. How do you approach a production incident without making it worse?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Stabilize user impact first, make small reversible changes, preserve evidence and coordinate clearly.

#### Interview answer

I start by confirming impact and severity, then I declare or join the incident process if needed. I avoid random changes, assign roles, capture the timeline and focus on mitigation: rollback, disable a feature, shed traffic, scale safely or route around a broken dependency. Root cause can wait if the system is still degrading.

#### Deep dive

A good incident response separates triage, mitigation and later root-cause analysis. During the incident, every action should have an owner, expected effect and rollback path.

#### Production angle

The worst production mistakes often come from uncoordinated fixes: multiple people changing config, restarting pods or scaling services without shared state.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not chase root cause while users are still heavily impacted.
- Do not restart everything before collecting the evidence needed to understand the failure.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What is the first goal during a serious production incident?
A: Stop or reduce user impact safely.

</details>

<a id="prod-fund-002"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>PROD-FUND-002. What information should be collected during incident triage?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Collect impact, scope, timeline, recent changes, telemetry, current mitigations and owner decisions.

#### Interview answer

I want to know what is failing, who is affected, when it started, whether it is still ongoing, and what changed around that time. Then I collect service metrics, logs, traces, deployment markers, config and feature-flag changes, dependency health, resource saturation and known workarounds. I also record actions already taken and their effects.

#### Deep dive

Triage data should support decisions, not become an endless evidence dump. The key questions are: impact, scope, likely layer, immediate mitigation and risk of further damage.

#### Production angle

A clear triage snapshot lets another engineer join the incident quickly and prevents repeated investigation.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not collect sensitive data casually in incident notes.
- Do not ignore business metrics; infrastructure can look healthy while checkout or payments are broken.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What are the minimum incident triage dimensions?
A: Impact, scope, timeline, recent changes and current evidence.

</details>

<a id="prod-fund-003"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>PROD-FUND-003. How do you distinguish symptoms from root causes?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Symptoms are observed failures; root causes explain why those failures happened and what would prevent recurrence.

#### Interview answer

I treat alerts and user reports as symptoms: high latency, 5xx errors, consumer lag, OOM kills or stale data. Then I ask what changed, where the failure begins, and which hypothesis explains all observations. For example, connection pool exhaustion may be a symptom of slow SQL, missing timeouts or a downstream outage, not the root cause itself.

#### Deep dive

A useful root cause survives disconfirming evidence and explains timing, scope and mechanism. Often there are multiple contributing causes: bad code, missing guardrails and weak observability.

#### Production angle

Mitigation may target a symptom, but prevention needs the mechanism and contributing conditions.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not call the first visible error the root cause.
- Do not force a single root cause when the incident required several failures to line up.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Is connection pool exhaustion always a root cause?
A: No. It is often a symptom of slow or stuck work.

</details>

<a id="prod-fund-004"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>PROD-FUND-004. How do you decide whether to mitigate first or keep investigating?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Mitigate first when user impact is high, damage is growing, data is at risk or a safe reversible action exists.

#### Interview answer

I compare impact, confidence and reversibility. If a new release correlates strongly with high errors and rollback is safe, I roll back before proving every detail. If data corruption may be ongoing, I may disable writes or pause consumers. If the incident is low impact or mitigation is risky, I gather more evidence first.

#### Deep dive

Good mitigation does not mean blind action. It means choosing the safest action likely to reduce impact while preserving enough evidence for later analysis.

#### Production angle

Senior engineers are expected to protect users and data, not win a debugging puzzle during the outage.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not roll back blindly when database schema or data changes make rollback unsafe.
- Do not keep investigating when a safe kill switch would stop active harm.

#### Optional Anki

Q: When should mitigation beat root-cause analysis?
A: When impact or damage is high and a safe mitigation exists.

</details>

<a id="prod-fund-005"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>PROD-FUND-005. What recent changes should you check during an incident?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Check code deployments, configuration, feature flags, schema changes, traffic shifts, infrastructure changes and dependency releases.

#### Interview answer

I check application versions, container images, Kubernetes manifests, config and secret changes, feature-flag rollouts, database migrations, Kafka schema or topic changes, dependency upgrades, traffic routing, autoscaling events and platform incidents. I also check business or traffic changes, such as a campaign or large customer batch.

#### Deep dive

"What changed?" is not only code. Runtime configuration, credentials, certificates, routing, data volume and downstream behavior can change without an application deployment.

#### Production angle

Deployment markers and audited config changes make this question answerable in minutes instead of guesswork.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not assume "no deploy" means "nothing changed."
- Do not ignore external provider, certificate and DNS changes.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Name three non-code changes that can cause incidents.
A: Configuration, traffic routing and database migrations.

</details>

<a id="prod-fund-006"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>PROD-FUND-006. How do you use logs, metrics and traces together during triage?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Use metrics for scope and trends, traces for request path timing, and logs for specific events and error details.

#### Interview answer

I start with metrics to see when the issue began, how widespread it is and which dimensions are affected. Then I use traces to find where time or errors occur across services and dependencies. Finally, I use logs with trace IDs, request IDs or safe business IDs to inspect exact exceptions, decisions and payload metadata.

#### Deep dive

No signal is enough alone. Metrics can hide individual failures, traces can be sampled or incomplete, and logs can be noisy or missing context.

#### Production angle

Good triage moves between aggregate and specific evidence: dashboard to trace to log line, then back to impact.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not rely on averages when p95 or p99 is failing.
- Do not log raw sensitive payloads just to make incidents easier.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What does each signal do best?
A: Metrics show scope, traces show path, logs show details.

</details>

<a id="prod-fund-007"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>PROD-FUND-007. How do you communicate status during an incident?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Communicate impact, current hypothesis, mitigation, next update time and explicit decisions in one shared channel.

#### Interview answer

I keep updates factual and short: what is affected, when it started, what users see, what we are doing, what changed and when the next update will come. I separate confirmed facts from hypotheses. Internally, I record decisions and owners; externally, I avoid speculation and promise only what we know.

#### Deep dive

Communication is part of incident control. It prevents duplicated work, gives stakeholders confidence and creates a usable timeline.

#### Production angle

Good updates reduce interruptions to responders because people can see state without asking privately.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not hide uncertainty; label it clearly.
- Do not let every engineer broadcast separate theories to stakeholders.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What should every incident status update include?
A: Impact, action, owner or next step, and next update time.

</details>

<a id="prod-fund-008"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>PROD-FUND-008. What should you avoid doing during an active production incident?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Avoid uncoordinated changes, destructive commands, broad restarts, noisy debugging and risky experiments without rollback.

#### Interview answer

I avoid making several changes at once, restarting services before evidence is captured, changing unknown configuration, increasing retries, scaling consumers into a saturated database, running heavy diagnostics blindly or bypassing safety checks. If an action can corrupt data or widen blast radius, it needs explicit approval and a rollback plan.

#### Deep dive

Some actions improve one metric while making the incident worse. For example, adding consumers may reduce Kafka lag only by overloading the database.

#### Production angle

Incident response is controlled change under pressure. The control matters as much as the change.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not turn on verbose logging globally under peak load without cost review.
- Do not kill a database blocker without understanding rollback and business impact.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Why avoid multiple simultaneous changes during an incident?
A: They make effects impossible to attribute and can widen damage.

</details>

<a id="prod-fund-009"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>PROD-FUND-009. How do you verify that the user impact is real and still ongoing?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Check user-facing SLIs, synthetic probes, real traffic, support reports and business outcome metrics.

#### Interview answer

I verify impact with external probes, gateway metrics, API error and latency SLIs, affected customer reports, conversion or order metrics, and service-specific business signals. I compare current data with baseline and segment by endpoint, tenant, region, version and client. Then I check whether the issue is still ongoing or only delayed alerts.

#### Deep dive

Infrastructure alerts should be tied back to user-visible behavior. A pod restart may be harmless; a small error rate on payment confirmation may be critical.

#### Production angle

Impact verification drives severity, paging, stakeholder communication and whether to freeze releases.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not declare recovery from pod health alone.
- Do not ignore low-volume critical flows because aggregate traffic looks normal.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What proves recovery better than pod readiness?
A: User-facing SLIs and business outcome metrics.

</details>

<a id="prod-fund-010"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>🧠 PROD-FUND-010. How do you handle an incident when telemetry is incomplete or misleading?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Use multiple independent signals, direct checks, controlled experiments and conservative mitigation while documenting telemetry gaps.

#### Interview answer

If telemetry is incomplete, I avoid over-trusting one dashboard. I compare client-side errors, gateway logs, application logs, database state, dependency status, Kubernetes events and manual probes. I may run a safe synthetic request or inspect a specific pod directly. After mitigation, I add follow-up work for the missing signal.

#### Deep dive

Telemetry can be delayed, sampled, dropped, mislabeled or broken by the same outage. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.

#### Production angle

Senior responders need enough system understanding to operate when dashboards are wrong or silent.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not add noisy emergency instrumentation that worsens the incident.
- Do not treat missing traces as proof that a dependency was not called.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What do you do when one telemetry source disagrees with user reports?
A: Cross-check with independent signals and direct probes.

</details>
