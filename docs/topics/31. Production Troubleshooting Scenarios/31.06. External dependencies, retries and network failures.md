# External dependencies, retries and network failures

---

<a id="prod-dep-001"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>PROD-DEP-001. An external dependency starts timing out. What do you check first?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Check dependency health, timeout settings, request volume, error classes, network path and whether your service is amplifying the issue.

#### Interview answer

I inspect client metrics by dependency: latency, timeout rate, retry attempts, connection pool waits, circuit breaker state and status codes. Then I check provider status, DNS/TLS errors, regional routing, recent config changes and whether timeout values cover connection acquisition, connect, TLS and read phases. I also check user impact and whether the dependency is critical or optional.

#### Deep dive

Timeouts can mean the dependency is slow, the network path is broken, the client pool is exhausted or the caller deadline is too short.

#### Production angle

Mitigation may be fail-fast behavior, fallback, circuit breaking, reduced concurrency, disabling optional features or provider escalation.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not retry non-idempotent operations blindly.
- Do not let one dependency exhaust all request threads.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What client metric must be separated during dependency incidents?
A: Original calls versus retry attempts.

</details>

<a id="prod-dep-002"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>PROD-DEP-002. A downstream service is slow, and your service is also failing. How do you limit blast radius?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Use timeouts, circuit breakers, bulkheads, rate limits, fallback and feature disablement to protect your service.

#### Interview answer

I first identify whether the downstream is critical or optional. Then I reduce concurrency to it, enforce deadlines, open or respect circuit breakers, shed optional work, disable the feature if possible and make responses degrade explicitly. I also isolate pools so unrelated endpoints do not wait behind the failing dependency.

#### Deep dive

Blast radius grows when one slow dependency consumes shared threads, connections, memory or retry budget.

#### Production angle

The goal is not to pretend the dependency is fine; it is to keep unrelated service functionality available.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not increase client timeouts during overload without checking thread and pool impact.
- Do not let fallback return incorrect critical data.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What pattern isolates one failing dependency from the whole service?
A: Bulkhead.

</details>

<a id="prod-dep-003"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>PROD-DEP-003. A retry storm starts after a dependency outage. How do you stop it?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Throttle or disable retries, add backoff/jitter, open circuit breakers, shed load and reduce producer concurrency.

#### Interview answer

I check where retries happen: clients, gateways, service code, Kafka consumers, schedulers or batch jobs. Then I reduce retry concurrency, disable unsafe retry policies, increase backoff with jitter, respect `Retry-After`, open circuit breakers and pause non-critical workers. I also separate original demand from retry attempts to measure recovery.

#### Deep dive

A retry storm can continue even after the dependency is partially recovered because queued retries keep pressure high.

#### Production angle

Retry budgets and per-dependency dashboards should make this visible before pools and threads are exhausted.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not retry at every layer of a call chain.
- Do not let Kafka consumers retry a failing downstream at full concurrency.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What two retry settings reduce synchronization?
A: Backoff and jitter.

</details>

<a id="prod-dep-004"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>PROD-DEP-004. How do you tell whether retries are helping or making the incident worse?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Compare retry success rate, added latency, downstream load, pool saturation and final user success.

#### Interview answer

I inspect first-attempt success, retry-attempt count, retry success rate, total dependency QPS, p99 latency, timeout rate, caller deadline exhaustion and final request success. Retries help when failures are transient and success improves within the latency budget. They hurt when they increase load, delay failures, duplicate side effects or consume scarce resources.

#### Deep dive

Retries should have bounded attempts, jitter, deadlines and idempotency rules.

#### Production angle

If retries are not improving final success rate, turn them down before they cause cascading failure.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not measure only "retry eventually succeeded" without user latency.
- Do not retry timed-out writes without idempotency keys or reconciliation.

#### Optional Anki

Q: When are retries harmful?
A: When they add load and latency without improving final success.

</details>

<a id="prod-dep-005"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>PROD-DEP-005. A circuit breaker is open. What should you inspect before closing it?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Check dependency recovery, failure classification, traffic level, half-open behavior, fallback impact and breaker thresholds.

#### Interview answer

I verify whether the dependency is actually healthy, whether failures are still happening, and whether the breaker counted the right errors. I check half-open probe behavior, current request volume, retry pressure, fallback correctness and recent config changes. Closing a breaker manually can flood a recovering dependency.

#### Deep dive

Circuit breakers are modal: changing state affects traffic behavior, not just observability.

#### Production angle

Recovery should be gradual: limited probes, rate limits and close monitoring before full traffic resumes.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not close the breaker just because the provider status page is green.
- Do not count business validation errors as dependency failures.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Why is manually closing a circuit breaker risky?
A: It can send full traffic to a dependency that is not ready.

</details>

<a id="prod-dep-006"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>PROD-DEP-006. DNS, TLS or certificate errors appear after deployment. What do you check?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Check target host, DNS resolution, truststore, certificate chain, hostname verification, time, proxy and image/config changes.

#### Interview answer

I compare old and new pods for JVM truststore, base image, certificates, DNS config, environment variables, proxy settings, service mesh sidecars and target URLs. I inspect exact exception types: unknown host, handshake failure, expired certificate, hostname mismatch or trust path failure. I also check system time because clock skew breaks TLS validation.

#### Deep dive

Certificate issues often come from base image changes, secret rotation, missing intermediate CA, wrong SNI or environment-specific endpoints.

#### Production angle

Mitigation may be rollback, certificate rotation fix, truststore update or routing traffic away from the bad environment.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not disable hostname verification or trust all certificates as a production fix.
- Do not log private keys or full secret material.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What should you never do as a TLS production fix?
A: Disable certificate validation broadly.

</details>

<a id="prod-dep-007"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>PROD-DEP-007. Only one availability zone or region has dependency failures. What do you inspect?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Inspect regional routing, DNS, network policy, credentials, endpoint config, provider status and local capacity.

#### Interview answer

I compare failing and healthy regions for dependency endpoint, DNS answers, TLS certificates, network ACLs, service mesh config, secrets, provider region, load balancer target health and recent rollouts. I also check whether local capacity, NAT gateways, egress IP allowlists or rate limits differ.

#### Deep dive

Regional failures are often configuration, routing, network or provider capacity issues rather than application code.

#### Production angle

Mitigation may be shifting traffic, disabling the affected region, failing over the dependency or reducing non-critical calls there.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not assume all regions run identical config.
- Do not fail over traffic without checking whether the target region has capacity.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What regional dependency issue is often overlooked?
A: Egress IP allowlists or regional DNS routing.

</details>

<a id="prod-dep-008"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>PROD-DEP-008. Redis or another cache is unavailable. How should the service degrade?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Fail open only for safe cached reads; protect the database and fail closed for correctness-critical cache uses.

#### Interview answer

I first classify cache use: performance cache, session store, rate limiter, lock, idempotency store or permission cache. For optional read caches, bypassing may be fine if the database can handle load. For sessions, locks, idempotency, permissions or rate limits, failing open can break correctness or security. I use short timeouts, circuit breakers and load shedding to protect the source of truth.

#### Deep dive

Cache outage can become database outage if every request falls through at full traffic.

#### Production angle

Cache degradation behavior should be designed before the incident, not invented while Redis is down.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not bypass a cache that enforces idempotency or authorization without review.
- Do not let cache retries overload Redis during recovery.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What is dangerous about bypassing a failed cache?
A: It can overload the database or break correctness/security.

</details>

<a id="prod-dep-009"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>PROD-DEP-009. A feature flag change causes unexpected production behavior. How do you investigate?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Check who changed the flag, rollout scope, targeting rules, default values, cache propagation and affected code paths.

#### Interview answer

I inspect audit logs, flag value, targeting rules, percentage rollout, environment, tenant/user attributes and effective values seen by each replica. Then I compare errors and latency by flag state, version and user group. I also check whether the flag controls irreversible side effects, schema assumptions or authorization logic.

#### Deep dive

Flags are runtime changes, so they can create incidents without deployment markers unless flag state is observable.

#### Production angle

Mitigation is often faster than code rollback: disable the flag, narrow targeting or activate a kill switch.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not use client-side flags for server-side authorization.
- Do not forget stale cached flag values across replicas.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What must feature flag systems expose for incidents?
A: Audit trail and effective flag state.

</details>

<a id="prod-dep-010"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>PROD-DEP-010. A dependency recovers, but your service stays degraded. What do you inspect?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Check queued work, retry backlog, open breakers, stale DNS/connections, pool state, caches and partial data damage.

#### Interview answer

After dependency recovery, I check whether our service still has saturated thread pools, full connection pools, retry queues, Kafka lag, open circuit breakers, stale fallback cache, DNS cache, bad keep-alive connections or delayed scheduled jobs. I also inspect whether failed attempts created partial state that now needs repair or replay.

#### Deep dive

Recovery is not instantaneous if the outage created backlog or local degraded state.

#### Production angle

The service may need controlled drain, breaker half-open probes, consumer throttling, cache invalidation or reconciliation before declaring recovery.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not declare recovery from provider status alone.
- Do not let backlog drain overload the recovered dependency again.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Why can a service stay degraded after a dependency recovers?
A: Local backlog, saturated pools, open breakers or stale state remain.

</details>

<a id="prod-dep-011"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>🧠 PROD-DEP-011. How can fallback logic return incorrect data or hide a real outage?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Fallbacks can serve stale, incomplete or unsafe data and make dashboards look healthier than user reality.

#### Interview answer

Fallback is safe only when the domain accepts degraded data. Returning stale recommendations may be fine; returning stale permissions, prices, balances or payment status can be dangerous. I check whether fallback use is visible in metrics, logs and responses, whether data freshness is bounded, and whether users or callers can distinguish degraded results.

#### Deep dive

Fallbacks should preserve correctness first, availability second. Silent fallback can hide a dependency outage until bad business decisions occur.

#### Production angle

Production systems need fallback counters, freshness indicators and alerts on sustained fallback usage.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not use fallback data for authorization or financial decisions unless explicitly designed.
- Do not hide degraded responses as fully successful results.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What must fallback telemetry show?
A: When fallback was used and how stale or degraded the result is.

</details>
