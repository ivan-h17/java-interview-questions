# Recovery, rollback and prevention

---

<a id="prod-rec-001"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>PROD-REC-001. How do runbooks help during incidents?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Runbooks reduce guesswork by documenting symptoms, checks, commands, owners, risks and recovery steps.

#### Interview answer

A useful runbook tells responders how to confirm impact, where dashboards and logs are, what common causes are, which mitigations are safe, who owns dependencies and what actions require approval. It should include rollback steps, verification checks and known risks, not just command snippets.

#### Deep dive

Runbooks should be tested and updated after incidents. A stale runbook can be worse than none if it gives false confidence.

#### Production angle

In high-pressure incidents, runbooks help less experienced on-call engineers act consistently and escalate early.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not include destructive commands without warnings and approval criteria.
- Do not let runbooks drift from current architecture.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What makes a runbook useful?
A: It gives safe, current, verifiable steps for common incidents.

</details>

<a id="prod-rec-002"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>PROD-REC-002. What should a rollback decision consider?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Consider user impact, confidence, reversibility, data/schema compatibility, side effects and availability of a safer mitigation.

#### Interview answer

I ask whether the release is likely responsible, whether rollback will reduce impact, and whether rollback is safe with current database schema, data written, config changes, feature flags, messages and external side effects. I also check whether a feature flag disable, traffic shift or roll-forward patch is safer.

#### Deep dive

Rollback is easy for stateless code changes and harder for stateful changes that already modified data or contracts.

#### Production angle

Rollback decisions should be explicit, timestamped and followed by verification.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not roll back code that is incompatible with already-applied schema changes.
- Do not delay rollback when the blast radius is high and rollback is known safe.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Why can rollback be unsafe?
A: State, schema or external side effects may have moved forward.

</details>

<a id="prod-rec-003"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>PROD-REC-003. When is rollback unsafe or insufficient?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Rollback is unsafe or insufficient when data, schema, contracts, external side effects or queued work cannot go back cleanly.

#### Interview answer

Rollback may fail if the new version wrote data old code cannot read, applied irreversible migrations, emitted incompatible events, changed external provider state or started background work that continues. It is insufficient if bad data already propagated, caches are stale, queues contain bad messages or clients have adopted a new contract.

#### Deep dive

Roll-forward or targeted repair may be safer than trying to recreate the previous world.

#### Production angle

Deployment plans should include compatibility windows and rollback points before destructive changes.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not confuse code rollback with data repair.
- Do not forget background workers and consumers after rollback.

#### Optional Anki

Q: When is roll-forward often safer?
A: After irreversible data/schema changes.

</details>

<a id="prod-rec-004"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>PROD-REC-004. How do you verify that rollback or mitigation restored service?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Verify user-facing SLIs, business outcomes, affected dimensions, backlog drain and absence of new damage.

#### Interview answer

I compare error rate, latency, success rate, throughput and business metrics before and after mitigation for the affected endpoints, tenants, regions and versions. I check that dependency pressure is normal, queues and retries are draining, circuit breakers are stable, no new data corruption occurs and support/customer reports have stopped.

#### Deep dive

Restoring pod health is not enough; recovery must be measured from user and business perspective.

#### Production angle

Verification determines whether to close the incident, continue mitigation or escalate.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not declare recovery while backlog is still growing.
- Do not miss degraded fallback mode that hides real errors.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What should verify recovery?
A: User-facing SLIs and business outcome metrics.

</details>

<a id="prod-rec-005"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>PROD-REC-005. How do you decide whether to pause deployments during an incident?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Pause when deployment risk could worsen diagnosis, capacity, state compatibility or recovery.

#### Interview answer

I pause deployments if the incident affects shared infrastructure, if current state is unclear, if rollouts could hide evidence, if schema/config compatibility is risky or if responders need stable baselines. I may allow urgent mitigations or hotfixes through an incident commander with explicit approval and verification.

#### Deep dive

Deployment freezes should be scoped. Freezing every system for a small localized issue can block useful fixes.

#### Production angle

The goal is controlled change, not bureaucracy.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not block a safe mitigation just because a freeze exists.
- Do not keep deploying unrelated changes into an unstable shared dependency.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Why pause deployments during a major incident?
A: To reduce change noise and avoid worsening impact.

</details>

<a id="prod-rec-006"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>PROD-REC-006. What makes a useful incident timeline?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

It records timestamps for detection, impact, hypotheses, actions, decisions, mitigations, recovery and follow-up facts.

#### Interview answer

A useful timeline includes when the incident started, when it was detected, when users were affected, what changed, who took which action, what evidence was observed, when mitigations happened and when recovery was verified. It separates facts from hypotheses and uses absolute times with timezone.

#### Deep dive

The timeline is not a blame document; it is the backbone for understanding detection delay, response delay and recovery.

#### Production angle

Good timelines improve postmortems, audits and future incident training.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not reconstruct only from memory after the incident.
- Do not omit failed hypotheses; they explain why decisions were made.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What should incident timeline entries include?
A: Time, action/evidence, owner and outcome.

</details>

<a id="prod-rec-007"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>PROD-REC-007. What makes a useful postmortem?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

A useful postmortem explains impact, timeline, causes, detection gaps, response gaps and concrete preventive actions.

#### Interview answer

I expect a postmortem to include user impact, duration, severity, timeline, triggering event, contributing factors, what went well, what went poorly, where detection or mitigation failed, and action items with owners and due dates. It should explain the system behavior clearly enough that another team can learn from it.

#### Deep dive

A postmortem should identify process and technical conditions that allowed the incident, not just the immediate bug.

#### Production angle

Postmortems are only valuable if follow-up work is tracked and completed.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not stop at "developer introduced bug."
- Do not create vague actions like "be more careful."

#### Optional Anki

Q: What makes a postmortem action useful?
A: Specific owner, concrete change and due date.

</details>

<a id="prod-rec-008"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>PROD-REC-008. How do you keep a postmortem blameless but technically honest?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Focus on decisions, context, system conditions and missing guardrails rather than personal fault.

#### Interview answer

I describe what happened and why the actions made sense with the information available at the time. Then I examine why the system allowed a bad change, why detection was late, why rollback was hard and why safeguards failed. Being blameless does not mean being vague; it means technical honesty without personal accusation.

#### Deep dive

Human error is usually a symptom of weak tooling, unclear ownership, missing tests or unsafe process.

#### Production angle

Blameless culture encourages reporting and learning; technical honesty ensures real fixes happen.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not remove uncomfortable technical facts to protect feelings.
- Do not name-and-shame responders or authors.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What replaces blame in a good postmortem?
A: Analysis of conditions, decisions and guardrails.

</details>

<a id="prod-rec-009"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>PROD-REC-009. How do you turn an incident into preventive engineering work?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Convert missed detection, unsafe change paths and failure modes into tests, guardrails, observability and design fixes.

#### Interview answer

I start from what would have prevented, detected or mitigated the incident earlier. Follow-up work might include contract tests, migration safety checks, load shedding, better timeouts, idempotency, dashboards, alerts, runbook updates, canary checks, ownership changes or architecture simplification. Actions should be prioritized by recurrence risk and impact.

#### Deep dive

The best follow-up is often a systemic guardrail, not just fixing one bug instance.

#### Production angle

Preventive work should enter the normal backlog with priority, owner and verification criteria.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not add noisy alerts for every symptom.
- Do not over-engineer rare low-impact failures while ignoring recurring classes.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What question starts preventive follow-up?
A: What would have prevented or detected this earlier?

</details>

<a id="prod-rec-010"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>🧠 PROD-REC-010. How do you prioritize follow-up actions after a serious incident?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Prioritize by risk reduction, recurrence likelihood, user impact, cost, dependency and whether the action creates a guardrail.

#### Interview answer

I rank actions by how much they reduce future severity or probability. First come items that stop recurrence or data loss: compatibility tests, idempotency, safe rollback, kill switches, timeouts or migration guardrails. Then detection and response improvements: alerts, dashboards, runbooks and drills. I avoid filling the backlog with low-value cleanup that does not change risk.

#### Deep dive

Some actions are prerequisites for others, such as adding ownership before adding new alerts.

#### Production angle

Leadership should understand which follow-ups are reliability risk controls, not optional polish.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not prioritize easy actions over high-impact ones automatically.
- Do not leave all actions assigned to "the team" without owners.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What should drive post-incident priority?
A: Risk reduction and recurrence prevention.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>🧠 PROD-REC-011. How do you detect that the same incident class is recurring under different symptoms?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Look for shared mechanisms across incidents: overload, missing timeouts, unsafe migrations, weak idempotency or poor ownership.

#### Interview answer

I compare incident timelines, contributing factors and failed controls, not only alert names. Different symptoms like p99 latency, Kafka lag and DB pool exhaustion may share one mechanism: slow downstream calls plus unbounded retries. I tag incidents by failure class and review recurrence trends during reliability planning.

#### Deep dive

Recurring classes often hide behind different surfaces because modern systems fail through chains of dependencies.

#### Production angle

Detecting recurrence helps justify systemic fixes such as retry budgets, migration policy, load shedding or observability standards.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not treat every incident as unique if the same missing guardrail appears.
- Do not collapse distinct mechanisms just because symptoms look similar.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What reveals recurring incident classes?
A: Shared mechanisms and missing controls across postmortems.

</details>
