# Latency, errors and traffic anomalies

---

<a id="prod-lat-001"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>PROD-LAT-001. A service has a latency spike. What do you check first?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Check scope, recent changes, saturation, dependency latency, error rate and whether the spike affects p95 or p99.

#### Interview answer

I first determine when the spike started, which endpoints, regions, tenants, versions and pods are affected, and whether traffic changed. Then I check dependency latency, database queries, connection pools, executor queues, GC pauses, CPU throttling, retries and load balancer metrics. If it correlates with a release, config change or traffic shift, I evaluate rollback or disabling the changed path.

#### Deep dive

Latency is usually a symptom. The cause may be waiting in queues, slow downstream calls, locks, retries, garbage collection, cold caches or overloaded infrastructure.

#### Production angle

Fast scoping prevents broad fixes like scaling everything when only one endpoint or tenant is affected.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not use average latency as the only signal.
- Do not assume the slowest visible span includes queue or pool wait time.

#### Optional Anki

Q: First latency-spike question?
A: What scope and dimension is affected?

</details>

<a id="prod-lat-002"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>PROD-LAT-002. A service has high p99 latency but normal average latency. What does that suggest?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

It suggests a tail problem affecting a minority of requests, instances, tenants, dependencies or payloads.

#### Interview answer

Normal average latency means most requests are still fast. I would segment by endpoint, pod, region, tenant, payload size, dependency and version. Common causes are one slow database query, pool waits, lock contention, GC pauses, retry behavior, a hot partition, noisy neighbor effects or a few large requests.

#### Deep dive

Tail latency matters more in fan-out systems because one slow dependency call can make the whole request slow.

#### Production angle

Users do not experience averages. A customer hitting the p99 path sees a broken service even if dashboards look mostly green.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not aggregate away bad instances or tenants.
- Do not ignore queueing and connection acquisition time.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What does high p99 with normal average usually mean?
A: A tail-heavy issue affecting a subset of requests.

</details>

<a id="prod-lat-003"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>PROD-LAT-003. A service has a high 5xx rate after deployment. How do you investigate?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Compare old and new versions, classify errors, inspect traces/logs and check config, schema and dependency changes.

#### Interview answer

I check whether errors are isolated to the new version, a specific pod, endpoint or request type. Then I classify exceptions and status codes, inspect representative traces and logs, compare dependency failures, and check recent config, feature flags, database migrations, schema changes and external API contracts. If the deployment is clearly responsible and rollback is safe, I mitigate first.

#### Deep dive

Post-deploy 5xx can come from code bugs, missing config, incompatible schema, changed serialization, broken permissions, bad startup warmup or dependency behavior.

#### Production angle

Version-aware metrics and deployment annotations make this a quick decision instead of a long debate.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not roll back blindly if the new version already wrote incompatible data.
- Do not ignore errors from only one canary or region.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Why compare 5xx rate by version?
A: To confirm whether the new deployment is responsible.

</details>

<a id="prod-lat-004"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>PROD-LAT-004. A service has a high 4xx rate after a frontend release. What do you check?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Check changed request shape, validation, authentication, authorization, CSRF/CORS, API contract and client rollout scope.

#### Interview answer

I classify the 4xx statuses: `400`, `401`, `403`, `404`, `409`, `422` or `429` mean different things. Then I compare request bodies, headers, content type, auth tokens, cookies, CORS behavior, URL paths, API version, validation errors and rate-limit responses before and after the frontend release. I also check whether old and new frontend versions coexist.

#### Deep dive

A 4xx spike is often a contract issue, not a backend outage. The backend may be correctly rejecting requests that the new client sends incorrectly.

#### Production angle

Mitigation might be frontend rollback, backend compatibility patch, feature flag disablement or temporarily accepting both old and new request forms.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not treat all 4xx as harmless client errors.
- Do not log full invalid payloads if they contain sensitive data.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What does a 4xx spike after frontend release often indicate?
A: Client/API contract or auth behavior changed.

</details>

<a id="prod-lat-005"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>PROD-LAT-005. Throughput drops while traffic remains high. What are likely causes?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Likely causes include saturation, queueing, slow dependencies, pool exhaustion, locks, retries, GC, CPU throttling or load shedding.

#### Interview answer

I compare incoming requests with completed requests, in-flight work, queue depth, error rate, timeouts and resource usage. If traffic is high but completed throughput falls, the system may be waiting on database or HTTP pools, blocked on locks, retrying, GC-bound, CPU-throttled, overloaded or rejecting work. I also check upstream timeouts because clients may give up before the service responds.

#### Deep dive

Throughput collapse often means the service crossed a saturation threshold. After that point, more work can reduce completed work.

#### Production angle

Mitigation may require load shedding or throttling, not adding more queued work.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not confuse accepted traffic with successful throughput.
- Do not increase retries when capacity is already saturated.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What does traffic high but completions low suggest?
A: Saturation, waiting or overload.

</details>

<a id="prod-lat-006"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>PROD-LAT-006. One endpoint is slow while others are healthy. How do you narrow the cause?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Trace that endpoint’s code path, dependencies, queries, payloads, locks, cache behavior and recent changes.

#### Interview answer

I segment latency by endpoint and compare the slow endpoint against healthy ones on the same pods. Then I inspect traces, database queries, downstream calls, serialization, validation, authorization, cache hits, payload size and feature flags specific to that endpoint. If only one request shape is slow, I narrow by tenant, parameter or data distribution.

#### Deep dive

One endpoint can be slow because of an N+1 query, missing index, cold cache, large response, expensive permission check or a slow optional dependency.

#### Production angle

Endpoint-specific mitigation can be precise: disable one feature, cache one read path or rate-limit one expensive operation.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not scale the whole service before proving the bottleneck is shared.
- Do not ignore data-dependent slowness within the endpoint.

#### Optional Anki

Q: First step when only one endpoint is slow?
A: Compare its trace and dependencies with healthy endpoints.

</details>

<a id="prod-lat-007"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>PROD-LAT-007. Latency is high only for one tenant, region or customer group. What do you inspect?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Inspect data size, routing, configuration, feature flags, shard/partition placement, dependency locality and customer-specific traffic.

#### Interview answer

I check whether that group hits a different region, database shard, cache keyspace, Kafka partition, feature-flag state, configuration, authorization path or downstream provider. I also inspect data volume, unusual payloads, quota limits, noisy-neighbor effects and whether the tenant recently changed behavior.

#### Deep dive

Per-tenant incidents often come from data skew, hot keys, bad tenant-aware cache keys, shard imbalance or region-specific dependencies.

#### Production angle

Tenant-aware dashboards and safe identifiers help resolve high-value customer incidents quickly.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not expose tenant-sensitive data in logs or metrics.
- Do not average all tenants together and hide the affected group.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What common cause affects only one tenant?
A: Data skew, routing or tenant-specific configuration.

</details>

<a id="prod-lat-008"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>PROD-LAT-008. The application is healthy, but users still see timeouts. What else do you check?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Check clients, gateway, load balancer, CDN, service mesh, DNS, network, upstream timeouts and response streaming.

#### Interview answer

I compare application metrics with edge and client-side metrics. Users may time out at the browser, mobile client, API gateway, ingress, load balancer, service mesh or upstream caller before the app records an error. I check 499/502/503/504-style responses, connection resets, TLS handshakes, DNS, large responses and timeout mismatches.

#### Deep dive

The application may complete after the caller already gave up. That can create duplicate retries and side effects if idempotency is weak.

#### Production angle

End-to-end latency budgets must include every hop, not only controller execution time.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not trust green application health when edge SLIs are red.
- Do not ignore client timeout values and retry behavior.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Where can a timeout happen outside the app?
A: Client, gateway, ingress, load balancer or service mesh.

</details>

<a id="prod-lat-009"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>PROD-LAT-009. How do you distinguish app latency from network or load balancer latency?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Compare timestamps and metrics across client, edge, load balancer, ingress, app and dependency spans.

#### Interview answer

I line up request timing at each boundary: client timing, gateway access logs, load balancer target response time, ingress logs, application request metrics and traces. If load balancer latency is high but app handler time is low, the delay may be connection setup, queueing, network, TLS, target selection or upstream timeout. If app spans dominate, the issue is inside or below the service.

#### Deep dive

Clock skew and sampling can mislead, so prefer metrics emitted by each hop and compare distributions, not one request only.

#### Production angle

This distinction decides whether the app team, platform team or network/provider team should lead mitigation.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not assume missing app logs mean the request never arrived; logging may fail or be sampled.
- Do not ignore load balancer queue and connection metrics.

#### Optional Anki

Q: How do you separate edge latency from app latency?
A: Compare timing at each request boundary.

</details>

<a id="prod-lat-010"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>PROD-LAT-010. How do you verify whether a latency fix actually worked?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Compare before/after user-facing percentiles, error rates, saturation and affected dimensions under similar load.

#### Interview answer

I verify p50/p95/p99 latency, timeout rate, error rate, throughput and resource saturation for the affected endpoint, tenant, region and version. I compare against baseline and control groups if possible. I also check whether the fix moved pressure elsewhere, such as from app threads to database load.

#### Deep dive

A fix is not proven by one successful request or a single green pod. It must improve the impacted population and remain stable over enough traffic.

#### Production angle

Rollback success or mitigation success should be measured by user-visible SLIs, not only internal resource graphs.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not declare victory before retry backlog or queued work drains.
- Do not ignore degraded fallbacks that hide real failures.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What proves a latency fix?
A: Improved user-facing latency and error SLIs for the affected scope.

</details>

<a id="prod-lat-011"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>PROD-LAT-011. How can queueing delay hide from ordinary request traces?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Traces often start after work is dequeued, so time spent waiting for a thread, executor or connection can be invisible.

#### Interview answer

If instrumentation starts inside the controller or client call, it may miss queueing before the handler runs. Requests can wait in an ingress, servlet thread pool, executor queue, database connection pool, HTTP client pool or Kafka consumer backlog. I check queue depth, active threads, pool acquisition time, in-flight requests and edge timing to reveal hidden delay.

#### Deep dive

This is why a trace can show "fast" internal spans while users still see timeouts.

#### Production angle

Instrumenting queue time and pool wait time is essential for diagnosing saturation.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not trust traces alone when latency is high.
- Do not make queues unbounded; they convert overload into delayed failure.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Why can traces miss latency?
A: The request may wait before traced work begins.

</details>

<a id="prod-lat-012"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>PROD-LAT-012. How do you investigate overload when the service should shed load instead of queueing more work?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Look for saturation, growing queues, timeout waste and failed priority handling; then protect critical work with rejection or throttling.

#### Interview answer

I check in-flight requests, executor queues, connection pools, retry volume, CPU throttling, database load and upstream timeout rates. If work is queued longer than callers wait, the service is wasting capacity. I would reduce concurrency, reject optional work, return `429` or `503` where appropriate, disable expensive features, throttle producers or protect critical endpoints with bulkheads.

#### Deep dive

Load shedding is not failure; it is controlled degradation that prevents total collapse.

#### Production angle

During overload, preserving checkout or payment may matter more than serving recommendations, exports or background refreshes.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not blindly scale if the database or downstream is the bottleneck.
- Do not shed critical traffic without a documented priority model.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What is the purpose of load shedding?
A: Reject lower-priority work to preserve critical service capacity.

</details>

<a id="prod-lat-013"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>🧠 PROD-LAT-013. How do coordinated retries or client behavior create traffic amplification?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Many clients retrying at the same time can multiply load on an already degraded service.

#### Interview answer

If clients retry failed or slow calls without jitter, deadlines or retry budgets, one original request can become several attempts. When this happens across many clients, traffic increases exactly when capacity is lowest. I check retry attempt metrics, original-vs-retry labels, upstream timeout settings, `429`/`503` handling and whether each layer retries independently.

#### Deep dive

Traffic amplification can come from frontend retries, gateway retries, service retries, Kafka consumer retries and background jobs all reacting to the same failure.

#### Production angle

Mitigation can include disabling retries, adding jitter, returning `Retry-After`, opening circuit breakers, throttling producers or shedding load.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not count retry attempts as independent user demand.
- Do not retry non-idempotent operations without idempotency protection.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Why are synchronized retries dangerous?
A: They amplify load during an outage.

</details>
