# SLIs, SLOs, SLAs and error budgets

---

<a id="obs-slo-001"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>OBS-SLO-001. What are SLIs, SLOs and SLAs, and how do they differ?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

An SLI is a reliability measurement, an SLO is the target for that measurement, and an SLA is an external commitment with consequences.

#### Interview answer

An SLI might be "percentage of successful checkout requests under 500 ms." The SLO is the objective, such as 99.9% over 30 days. An SLA is a customer-facing or contractual promise, often with credits, penalties or formal obligations.

#### Deep dive

Good SLIs measure user-visible service behavior, not just infrastructure health. SLOs are engineering targets; SLAs should usually be looser than internal SLOs.

#### Production angle

SLOs help teams decide when reliability work should take priority over feature delivery.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not use CPU or pod count as the main SLI for user experience.
- Do not promise an SLA that the system is not engineered to meet.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What is an SLI?
A: A measurable indicator of service reliability.

</details>

<a id="obs-slo-002"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>OBS-SLO-002. How do you choose SLIs for an HTTP API?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Choose SLIs around availability, latency and correctness for user-visible API operations.

#### Interview answer

For an HTTP API, useful SLIs include the ratio of successful requests to total requests, percentage of requests under a latency threshold, and sometimes correctness or freshness. They should be measured by route or critical workflow, not only globally.

#### Deep dive

Define what counts as "good." For example, 2xx may be good, selected 4xx may be expected client errors, and 5xx or timeout may be bad. Latency should use threshold-based good events or meaningful percentiles.

#### Production angle

Good HTTP SLIs tell you when users are actually experiencing failed or slow operations.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not count every 4xx as service failure without understanding API semantics.
- Do not hide a failing critical endpoint inside global API availability.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What are common HTTP API SLI categories?
A: Availability, latency and correctness.

</details>

<a id="obs-slo-003"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>OBS-SLO-003. How do you choose SLIs for an asynchronous worker or message consumer?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Measure completion, processing delay, freshness, failure paths and backlog rather than only consumer uptime.

#### Interview answer

For a worker, useful SLIs include percentage of events processed successfully, oldest unprocessed event age, end-to-end processing latency, retry backlog age, DLQ rate and business outcome completion. Consumer lag is useful but not always enough.

#### Deep dive

Offset lag counts records, while time lag measures business delay. A small number of old payment events may be more important than a large backlog of low-priority analytics events.

#### Production angle

Async SLOs should answer whether business work is completed within the expected time.

#### Traps / caveats

- Lag of zero does not prove side effects succeeded.
- Producer clock skew can distort event-time latency.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Why is time lag often better than offset lag?
A: It maps backlog to user or business delay.

</details>

<a id="obs-slo-004"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>OBS-SLO-004. What is an error budget?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

An error budget is the allowed amount of unreliability within an SLO window.

#### Interview answer

If a service has a 99.9% monthly availability SLO, the error budget is the remaining 0.1% of requests or time that may be bad during that month. It turns reliability into a budget that can be spent by incidents, risky changes or expected failures.

#### Deep dive

Error budgets are more useful when based on event counts or good/bad ratios than vague uptime claims. They should be tied to meaningful SLIs.

#### Production angle

When the budget is burning too fast, teams may slow releases and prioritize reliability fixes.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not treat error budget as permission to ignore preventable incidents.
- Do not compute a budget from a poor SLI.

#### Optional Anki

Q: For a 99.9% SLO, what is the error budget percentage?
A: 0.1%.

</details>

<a id="obs-slo-005"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>OBS-SLO-005. How do error budgets influence release decisions?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

They help decide when to continue feature releases and when to focus on reliability.

#### Interview answer

If a service is comfortably within budget, the team may accept normal release risk. If the budget is mostly consumed or burning quickly, the team should reduce risky changes, fix reliability issues, improve rollback safety or add capacity before shipping more risk.

#### Deep dive

Error-budget policies should be explicit. They can define thresholds for release freezes, extra review, postmortem actions or reliability sprints.

#### Production angle

This prevents reliability decisions from becoming purely subjective arguments during pressure.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not freeze all work automatically if the remaining work is needed to fix reliability.
- Do not let teams game SLO definitions to avoid release pressure.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What does a fast-burning error budget usually signal?
A: Reliability risk that may justify slowing risky releases.

</details>

<a id="obs-slo-006"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>OBS-SLO-006. Why should SLOs be user-centered instead of infrastructure-centered?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Users care whether their operations succeed quickly and correctly, not whether internal components look healthy.

#### Interview answer

Infrastructure metrics are useful diagnostics, but an SLO should represent customer-visible service quality. CPU, memory or pod readiness can be green while users still see timeouts, stale data or failed payments.

#### Deep dive

A user-centered SLO might measure successful checkout completion, event freshness or API response under a threshold. Infrastructure metrics explain why the SLO is failing.

#### Production angle

User-centered SLOs reduce false confidence from healthy infrastructure during real user-impacting incidents.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not alert only on CPU while ignoring failed business flows.
- Infrastructure SLOs can still be useful for platform teams, but they are not product reliability by themselves.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What should an application SLO primarily represent?
A: User-visible service quality.

</details>

<a id="obs-slo-007"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>OBS-SLO-007. How do you avoid setting unrealistic SLOs?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Base SLOs on user needs, historical performance, dependency limits and the cost of reliability.

#### Interview answer

I would start from the user journey and business impact, then inspect historical data and dependency behavior. The SLO should be strict enough to protect users but realistic enough that the team can meet it without unsustainable cost.

#### Deep dive

Very high SLOs require investment in redundancy, automation, testing, capacity and incident response. They also leave tiny error budgets, which may slow delivery.

#### Production angle

An unrealistic SLO creates constant alerts and policy violations without improving the system.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not copy another company's SLO blindly.
- Do not set SLOs without enough measurement data.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What inputs help define a realistic SLO?
A: User expectations, historical data, dependency limits and reliability cost.

</details>

<a id="obs-slo-008"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>OBS-SLO-008. What is burn-rate alerting?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Burn-rate alerting pages when the service is consuming its error budget too quickly.

#### Interview answer

Burn rate compares current bad-event rate with the rate allowed by the SLO. A burn rate of 1 means the service would use exactly its budget over the SLO window. Higher burn rates mean the budget will be exhausted faster.

#### Deep dive

Multi-window, multi-burn-rate alerts combine short and long windows. This catches severe incidents quickly while reducing alerts for brief harmless spikes.

#### Production angle

Burn-rate alerts align paging with real reliability risk instead of arbitrary CPU or error thresholds.

#### Traps / caveats

- Single short windows can be noisy.
- Long windows alone can reset slowly after recovery.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What does burn rate measure?
A: How fast the service is consuming error budget relative to the SLO.

</details>

<a id="obs-slo-009"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>🧠 OBS-SLO-009. Why are low-traffic services tricky for SLO alerting?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Small request counts make error ratios noisy and can cause either false pages or missed impact.

#### Interview answer

In a low-traffic service, one failed request can produce a huge error percentage, while a serious outage may take time to generate enough events to trigger a ratio-based alert. Alerting needs minimum traffic, longer windows, synthetic checks or business-specific signals.

#### Deep dive

Low-traffic SLOs often need a mix of event-based SLIs, synthetic probes and ticket-level alerts. The right approach depends on whether one failed request is critical.

#### Production angle

An internal admin API and a payment callback endpoint may both be low traffic, but they need very different alerting behavior.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not page on one harmless failure for a rarely used endpoint.
- Do not ignore low-volume critical workflows just because aggregate traffic is small.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Why can one failure distort a low-traffic SLO?
A: The denominator is small, so the error ratio jumps sharply.

</details>
