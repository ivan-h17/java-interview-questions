# Consistency, transactions and distributed correctness

---

<a id="sd-cons-001"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>SD-CONS-001. What is CAP theorem, and how should it be discussed pragmatically?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

CAP says that during a network partition, a distributed system must choose between consistency and availability.

#### Interview answer

CAP is often misused. It does not mean every system always picks only two of three properties. The practical point is that when nodes cannot communicate, the system must decide whether to reject or delay operations to preserve consistency, or accept operations and reconcile later to preserve availability.

#### Deep dive

In real design, talk about concrete operations. Account transfers may choose consistency over availability. Social feed likes may accept temporary inconsistency. A system can make different choices for different flows.

#### Production angle

CAP discussions should lead to user-visible behavior during partitions, retries, reconciliation, and alerting.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not say a database is simply "CA" without discussing partitions.
- Do not use CAP as a substitute for concrete consistency requirements.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What is the practical CAP trade-off?
A: During a partition, choose whether to preserve consistency or keep accepting requests.

</details>

<a id="sd-cons-002"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>SD-CONS-002. What is eventual consistency?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Eventual consistency means replicas or derived views may be temporarily stale but converge if updates stop and propagation succeeds.

#### Interview answer

In an eventually consistent system, a write may not be visible everywhere immediately. For example, a profile update may appear in one service before a search index or cache sees it. This is acceptable when short staleness is tolerable and the system has propagation, retry, and repair mechanisms.

#### Deep dive

Eventual consistency is not an excuse for random inconsistency. The design should define convergence rules, conflict handling, maximum acceptable lag, and user experience during delay.

#### Production angle

Monitor replication lag, event delay, stale reads, failed consumers, and reconciliation results.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not use eventual consistency for flows that require immediate correctness unless the business accepts it.
- Do not forget repair paths when propagation fails.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What does eventual consistency promise?
A: Data converges over time if updates stop and propagation succeeds.

</details>

<a id="sd-cons-003"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>SD-CONS-003. What is strong consistency, and when is it required?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Strong consistency means reads observe the latest committed write according to the system's consistency model.

#### Interview answer

Strong consistency is needed when stale data can violate business rules or user trust: balances, inventory reservation, permissions, unique usernames, payment state, and critical workflow transitions. It usually requires reading from the authoritative store, coordination, locks, transactions, or consensus depending on the system.

#### Deep dive

Strong consistency has costs: higher latency, lower availability during partitions, less caching freedom, and more coordination. It should be applied to invariants that need it, not every screen by default.

#### Production angle

Senior design identifies which data needs strong consistency and which can be eventually consistent for performance and availability.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not send strongly consistent reads to lagging replicas.
- Do not require strong consistency for all analytics, feeds, or notifications without business need.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Give examples of strongly consistent data.
A: Balances, inventory reservations, permissions, payments, and unique constraints.

</details>

<a id="sd-cons-004"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>SD-CONS-004. What is read-after-write consistency?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Read-after-write consistency means a user can read their own successful write immediately.

#### Interview answer

If a user updates their profile and refreshes the page, they expect to see the new value. This can be ensured by reading from the primary, session stickiness, tracking replication position, invalidating caches, or returning the updated representation from the write operation.

#### Deep dive

Read-after-write is often needed per user or per session, not globally. Other users may still tolerate eventual consistency. This distinction allows better performance while preserving a sane user experience.

#### Production angle

Violating read-after-write expectations creates support tickets because users think their action failed.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not use stale caches after a write unless the UI accounts for it.
- Do not confuse read-after-write with global linearizability.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What is read-after-write consistency?
A: A user sees their own successful write on subsequent reads.

</details>

<a id="sd-cons-005"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>SD-CONS-005. What is idempotency, and why is it important in distributed systems?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Idempotency means repeating the same operation has the same effect as doing it once.

#### Interview answer

Distributed systems retry because networks, clients, and services fail ambiguously. A client may not know whether a timeout happened before or after the server committed the operation. Idempotent operations allow safe retries without duplicate orders, payments, emails, or state transitions.

#### Deep dive

Idempotency can be natural, like setting a value to a known state, or designed, like using an idempotency key for create or payment commands. The result returned on retry should usually match the original outcome.

#### Production angle

Idempotency is essential for payments, order creation, message consumers, webhooks, and external API calls.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not assume HTTP `POST` is safe to retry by default.
- Do not make only the controller idempotent while downstream side effects duplicate.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Why is idempotency important?
A: It makes retries safe after ambiguous failures.

</details>

<a id="sd-cons-006"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>SD-CONS-006. How do idempotency keys make retries safer?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

An idempotency key lets the server recognize repeated attempts of the same logical operation.

#### Interview answer

The client sends a unique key for a command, such as creating a payment. The server stores the key with request fingerprint, status, and result inside a durable store. If the same key is received again, the server returns the original result or current operation status instead of executing the command again.

#### Deep dive

The key must be scoped, usually by user, tenant, endpoint, or operation type. The server should reject the same key with a different request body to prevent accidental collisions or abuse.

#### Production angle

Idempotency-key stores need TTLs, unique constraints, cleanup, observability, and clear behavior for in-progress operations.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not store idempotency keys only in volatile local memory for critical operations.
- Do not allow the same key to mean different requests.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What should an idempotency-key record store?
A: Key, scope, request fingerprint, status, result, timestamps, and sometimes error state.

</details>

<a id="sd-cons-007"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>SD-CONS-007. How do you avoid double charging or duplicate order creation?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Use idempotency keys, database constraints, transactional state transitions, and careful external call handling.

#### Interview answer

For order creation or payment, I would generate or require an idempotency key, store the operation durably, and enforce uniqueness in the database. The state machine should prevent invalid duplicate transitions. Calls to payment providers should also use their idempotency mechanism if available.

#### Deep dive

The hard case is ambiguous failure: the app times out after sending a payment request. The recovery path should query operation state, reconcile with the provider, and avoid issuing a second charge blindly.

#### Production angle

Money flows need audit trails, reconciliation jobs, alerting for stuck states, and manual repair procedures.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not rely only on frontend button disabling.
- Do not retry payment calls without provider idempotency or reconciliation.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What protects against duplicate order creation?
A: Idempotency keys, unique constraints, durable operation state, and safe retries.

</details>

<a id="sd-cons-008"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>SD-CONS-008. How do distributed transactions differ from local database transactions?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

A local transaction covers one database resource; a distributed transaction coordinates multiple resources or services.

#### Interview answer

A local transaction can atomically update several tables in one database. A distributed transaction tries to coordinate changes across multiple databases, services, or resource managers. This is much harder because failures can happen between participants, and the system needs a coordination protocol.

#### Deep dive

Two-phase commit and XA can provide atomic commit across resources, but they add blocking behavior, operational complexity, and availability concerns. Many microservice designs prefer local transactions plus Outbox, Saga, idempotency, and reconciliation.

#### Production angle

Distributed transactions should not be introduced casually because they affect availability, operations, and failure recovery.

#### Traps / caveats

- A transaction in one service cannot roll back another service's already committed work.
- Avoid pretending synchronous HTTP calls are part of the same transaction.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What is the common alternative to distributed transactions in microservices?
A: Local transactions plus Saga, Outbox, idempotency, and reconciliation.

</details>

<a id="sd-cons-009"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>SD-CONS-009. When is a Saga-style workflow useful?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Saga is useful when a business workflow spans multiple services and cannot use one local transaction.

#### Interview answer

An order workflow might reserve inventory, authorize payment, create shipment, and send notification. Each step commits locally. If a later step fails, the system executes compensating actions, such as releasing inventory or canceling payment authorization. Saga manages long-running distributed workflows through orchestration or choreography.

#### Deep dive

Saga does not provide ACID isolation across services. It requires idempotent steps, clear state, retries, compensation, timeout handling, and monitoring.

#### Production angle

Saga workflows need visibility into stuck, failed, compensated, and manually repaired operations.

#### Traps / caveats

- Compensation is not always a true rollback.
- Choreography can become hard to understand if many services react to events blindly.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What problem does Saga solve?
A: Coordinating a long-running workflow across services using local transactions and compensation.

</details>

<a id="sd-cons-010"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>SD-CONS-010. How do database constraints and idempotency work together?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Idempotency handles retry semantics; database constraints enforce correctness under concurrency.

#### Interview answer

An idempotency key lets repeated requests map to one logical operation. A unique database constraint makes that guarantee race-safe when two requests arrive at the same time. For example, a unique constraint on `(tenant_id, idempotency_key)` or order business key prevents duplicate rows even if application checks race.

#### Deep dive

Application-level checks are not enough under concurrent requests. Constraints, transactions, and appropriate isolation are the final line of defense for invariants such as unique usernames, one active subscription, or one payment per order.

#### Production angle

Constraint violations should be handled as expected conflict cases, not only as unexpected database errors.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not implement uniqueness only with a pre-insert select.
- Do not forget to scope idempotency keys by tenant or user.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Why combine idempotency keys with unique constraints?
A: To make retry safety race-safe under concurrent requests.

</details>

<a id="sd-cons-011"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>SD-CONS-011. How do you handle conflicting updates in a distributed design?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Use ownership, versioning, locks, compare-and-set, merge rules, or conflict resolution based on business semantics.

#### Interview answer

First, define who owns the data and which operation is authoritative. For a single database, optimistic locking with a version column or pessimistic locking may be enough. Across services or replicas, conflicts may need business rules, last-write-wins only for safe fields, manual review, or event ordering by aggregate.

#### Deep dive

Conflict handling is domain-specific. Bank balances should not use last-write-wins. Profile display names might. Inventory reservation needs transactional constraints. Collaborative editing needs merge algorithms or operation logs.

#### Production angle

Conflicts should be observable and repairable, not silently hidden.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not use timestamp-based last-write-wins for critical financial state.
- Do not allow multiple services to own and mutate the same business fact independently.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What is optimistic locking?
A: Updating with an expected version and failing if another update changed the row first.

</details>

<a id="sd-cons-012"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>SD-CONS-012. When is consensus needed, and why is it expensive?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Consensus is needed when distributed nodes must agree on one value or leader despite failures.

#### Interview answer

Consensus is used for leader election, distributed configuration, locks, metadata, membership, and strongly consistent coordination. It is expensive because nodes must communicate, form quorums, handle partitions, and often trade latency or availability for agreement.

#### Deep dive

Systems such as ZooKeeper, etcd, and Consul provide consensus-backed coordination. Application code should use such systems carefully and avoid putting high-volume business writes through consensus unless truly required.

#### Production angle

Consensus systems are critical dependencies. They need careful capacity, backups, quorum placement, monitoring, and operational discipline.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not use consensus for ordinary cache invalidation or high-QPS counters without a strong reason.
- Do not deploy quorum members in one failure domain and expect high availability.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What does consensus provide?
A: Agreement among distributed nodes despite failures, usually using quorum-based coordination.

</details>

<a id="sd-cons-013"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>SD-CONS-013. Why are clocks and global ordering tricky in distributed systems?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Different machines do not share a perfectly synchronized clock, and messages can arrive late, duplicated, or out of order.

#### Interview answer

If two services update data at similar times, wall-clock timestamps may not prove which event happened first. Clock skew, network delay, retries, and asynchronous queues make global ordering hard. Designs often prefer per-entity ordering, version numbers, sequence numbers, database commit order, or broker partition order where applicable.

#### Deep dive

Global ordering is expensive because it requires coordination. Many systems only need ordering per aggregate, user, account, or partition. That is easier and usually enough for business correctness.

#### Production angle

Clock assumptions affect audit logs, conflict resolution, event processing, token expiry, and incident timelines.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not use local server timestamps as the only correctness mechanism for critical ordering.
- Kafka ordering is per partition, not global across all partitions.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Why is global ordering hard?
A: Clocks skew, networks delay messages, retries duplicate work, and global order requires coordination.

</details>
