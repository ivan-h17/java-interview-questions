# Problem framing, requirements and interview flow

---

<a id="sd-frame-001"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>SD-FRAME-001. How do you clarify functional requirements in a system design interview?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Clarify what the system must do, who uses it, and which workflows matter most.

#### Interview answer

Start by asking about users, core actions, input and output, business rules, permissions, and expected edge cases. For example, for an order system, clarify whether users can create, cancel, pay, refund, track, and retry orders. Then separate must-have flows from nice-to-have features so the design has a clear target.

#### Deep dive

Functional requirements define behavior. They should describe user-visible capabilities and system responsibilities, not implementation choices. A good interview answer narrows the problem enough to design something concrete within the available time.

#### Production angle

Missing a key flow usually causes the wrong data model, wrong API boundaries, or wrong consistency guarantees.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not start with Kafka, Redis, or Kubernetes before understanding the product flow.
- Do not accept a vague prompt as complete requirements.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What are functional requirements?
A: The user-visible behaviors and system responsibilities the design must support.

</details>

<a id="sd-frame-002"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>SD-FRAME-002. How do you clarify non-functional requirements before drawing an architecture?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Ask about scale, latency, availability, consistency, security, durability, cost, and operational constraints.

#### Interview answer

Non-functional requirements shape architecture choices. A chat system, banking transfer system, and analytics dashboard may have similar APIs but very different latency, consistency, durability, and availability needs. I would clarify expected traffic, peak load, data size, SLA or SLO targets, failure tolerance, geographic needs, compliance constraints, and budget sensitivity.

#### Deep dive

NFRs are often trade-offs. Strong consistency can increase latency. Multi-region availability can increase cost and operational complexity. Low latency can force caching, precomputation, locality, or asynchronous processing.

#### Production angle

Most production incidents happen around NFRs: overload, data loss, slow dependencies, unavailable regions, unsafe retries, or insufficient observability.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not treat every system as if it needs global scale.
- Do not claim five nines availability without explaining cost and dependencies.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Name common non-functional requirements in system design.
A: Scale, latency, availability, consistency, security, durability, observability, cost, and operability.

</details>

<a id="sd-frame-003"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>SD-FRAME-003. How do you identify the most important user flows or critical paths?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Find the flows that carry the main business value, highest traffic, highest risk, or strictest correctness needs.

#### Interview answer

I ask which actions users perform most often and which actions cannot fail silently. In an e-commerce system, browsing may dominate traffic, but checkout is the critical correctness path. In a notification system, accepting requests may be simple, but delivery, retry, and deduplication define reliability.

#### Deep dive

Critical paths are not always the highest-QPS paths. They can be money movement, inventory changes, account access, authentication, or a regulatory audit trail. The design should protect these paths first and allow lower-value paths to degrade when needed.

#### Production angle

Critical path thinking helps choose transaction boundaries, idempotency, monitoring, alerting, and fallback behavior.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not optimize a rare admin flow before the main user flow.
- Do not ignore low-QPS flows that carry high business risk.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What makes a flow critical?
A: High business value, high traffic, high correctness risk, or strict latency and availability requirements.

</details>

<a id="sd-frame-004"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>SD-FRAME-004. How do you decide which parts of the system are in scope for the interview?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Agree on what will be designed deeply and what will be treated as an external dependency or simplified component.

#### Interview answer

System design interviews are time-boxed, so I explicitly define scope. For example, while designing checkout, I may include cart, order, payment orchestration, inventory reservation, and notification events, but treat payment provider internals as external. I also state which features are out of scope, such as fraud, recommendations, or warehouse operations, unless the interviewer wants them.

#### Deep dive

Scope control shows engineering judgment. It keeps the answer focused and prevents shallow coverage of everything. The goal is not to draw every possible component, but to reason deeply about the system's main risks.

#### Production angle

In real projects, unclear scope leads to weak ownership, missing APIs, duplicated responsibilities, and designs that nobody can operate.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not exclude a component that owns a key invariant.
- Do not spend most of the interview on out-of-scope implementation details.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Why define scope in a system design interview?
A: To focus time on the important flows, risks, and trade-offs.

</details>

<a id="sd-frame-005"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>SD-FRAME-005. How do you define success metrics for a system design?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Define measurable targets for user behavior, latency, reliability, correctness, cost, and operations.

#### Interview answer

Success metrics turn vague goals into design constraints. For an API, I might define p95 latency, availability, error rate, request throughput, duplicate rate, stale read tolerance, and recovery time. For a queue-based workflow, I would also track backlog, processing delay, retry rate, and dead-letter volume.

#### Deep dive

Metrics should match the product. A feed optimizes read latency and freshness. A payment system optimizes correctness, idempotency, auditability, and controlled failure. A batch system may care more about completion time and data quality than per-request latency.

#### Production angle

Good metrics become dashboards, alerts, SLOs, capacity planning inputs, and release gates.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not define only traffic metrics and ignore correctness.
- Do not use averages when tail latency or error spikes are the real user problem.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What should success metrics cover?
A: User experience, performance, reliability, correctness, cost, and operability.

</details>

<a id="sd-frame-006"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>SD-FRAME-006. How do you communicate assumptions during a system design interview?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

State assumptions explicitly, explain why they are reasonable, and adjust them when the interviewer gives new constraints.

#### Interview answer

I would say something like: "I will assume 10 million daily users, 10 percent active at peak, read-heavy traffic, and no strict global consistency requirement unless we discover one." This makes the design reviewable. If the interviewer changes a number or requirement, I can update the affected components instead of pretending the assumption was a fact.

#### Deep dive

Assumptions are useful because system design often starts with incomplete information. They should be concrete enough to drive decisions but not so rigid that the design cannot adapt.

#### Production angle

Real architecture documents should also record assumptions, because teams need to revisit them when traffic, product behavior, or compliance requirements change.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not hide major assumptions inside the design.
- Do not defend an assumption after the interviewer corrects it.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Why state assumptions explicitly?
A: So the design can be reviewed, challenged, and adjusted when requirements change.

</details>

<a id="sd-frame-007"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>SD-FRAME-007. How do you structure a system design answer from requirements to trade-offs?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Use a flow: requirements, estimates, APIs, data model, high-level architecture, deep dives, failures, observability, and trade-offs.

#### Interview answer

I start by clarifying requirements and scale. Then I define core APIs and data entities, draw the main components, explain request and write paths, and deep dive into the riskiest parts. After that I discuss failure modes, consistency, security, observability, cost, and possible evolution.

#### Deep dive

The exact order can vary, but the answer should move from problem to design to validation. Seniority is visible when the design is tied back to requirements and trade-offs, not when it is just a diagram of popular tools.

#### Production angle

This structure resembles real design review: scope, constraints, proposed architecture, risk analysis, operational plan, and migration path.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not spend all the time on the diagram and skip correctness.
- Do not list components without explaining the paths through them.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What is a useful system design answer structure?
A: Requirements, estimates, APIs, data model, architecture, deep dives, failures, observability, and trade-offs.

</details>

<a id="sd-frame-008"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>SD-FRAME-008. How do you avoid jumping to technologies too early?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Start from requirements and access patterns, then choose technologies to satisfy specific constraints.

#### Interview answer

Instead of saying "use Kafka and Redis" immediately, I first ask whether the workflow is synchronous or asynchronous, whether data must be strongly consistent, how much traffic exists, and what failure behavior is acceptable. Then technology choices become justified: a relational database for transactional invariants, a cache for hot reads, a queue for decoupling slow work, or object storage for large files.

#### Deep dive

Technologies are implementation tools. The interviewer wants to see why a component exists and what trade-offs it introduces. A simple database-backed design may be stronger than a complex distributed design if the scale and requirements do not justify the complexity.

#### Production angle

Premature tool selection creates systems that are hard to operate and solve the wrong problem.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not pick NoSQL only because the prompt says "scale".
- Do not add a queue unless asynchronous behavior and delayed failure are acceptable.

#### Optional Anki

Q: How do you avoid premature technology choices?
A: Explain requirements and access patterns first, then map tools to concrete constraints.

</details>

<a id="sd-frame-009"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>SD-FRAME-009. How do you handle an interviewer who keeps changing requirements?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Treat changes as new constraints, restate their impact, and adapt the design deliberately.

#### Interview answer

I would pause and say what changed: "If writes must now be globally visible immediately, the consistency model changes and the previous cache-heavy design may not be enough." Then I would update the affected areas, such as data model, replication, latency, cost, or failure behavior. This shows that I understand the design is constraint-driven.

#### Deep dive

Changing requirements are common in senior interviews because they test flexibility. The right response is not to defend the first design, but to explain which assumptions no longer hold and what trade-off changes.

#### Production angle

Real systems evolve the same way. New compliance, traffic, or product requirements often require incremental redesign, migration plans, and risk controls.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not silently patch the diagram without explaining the trade-off.
- Do not restart from scratch unless the new requirement invalidates the core approach.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What should you do when requirements change mid-design?
A: Restate the new constraint, identify impacted assumptions, and adapt the design.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>SD-FRAME-010. How do you show seniority through trade-off discussion instead of naming tools?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Explain why each choice fits the requirements, what it costs, and how it fails.

#### Interview answer

A senior answer connects components to trade-offs. For example, caching reduces latency and database load, but introduces staleness, invalidation, and outage amplification risks. Sharding increases write capacity but complicates queries, migrations, operations, and rebalancing. The point is to show judgment, not to name the most advanced stack.

#### Deep dive

Seniority appears in constraint reasoning: consistency versus availability, latency versus durability, cost versus redundancy, simplicity versus scalability, and operational complexity versus product value.

#### Production angle

Teams live with trade-offs after the design review. A good architecture is one the team can build, debug, operate, and evolve.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not present every technology as an automatic best practice.
- Do not ignore operational ownership, migration, rollback, and observability.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What makes a system design answer senior?
A: Clear trade-offs, failure modes, operational thinking, and choices tied to requirements.

</details>
