# Design review, observability and common prompts

---

<a id="sd-review-001"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>SD-REVIEW-001. How do you review a system design for bottlenecks and failure modes?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Walk the critical read and write paths, then check capacity, shared dependencies, consistency, failure behavior, and observability.

#### Interview answer

I review each component on the path: client, gateway, service, cache, database, queue, workers, and external dependencies. For each one I ask: can it handle peak load, what happens if it is slow or down, is there a timeout, can retries duplicate work, and how will we detect failure?

#### Deep dive

A good review also checks data ownership, schema evolution, security boundaries, tenant isolation, deployment safety, and recovery procedures. The goal is to find the first bottleneck and the highest-risk failure modes.

#### Production angle

This review becomes the basis for load tests, dashboards, alerts, runbooks, and rollout plans.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not review only the happy path.
- Do not forget background workers and asynchronous workflows.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What paths should a system design review trace?
A: Critical read paths, write paths, failure paths, and recovery paths.

</details>

<a id="sd-review-002"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>SD-REVIEW-002. What metrics, logs and traces should a new design expose?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Expose request, dependency, resource, business, and workflow metrics with correlated logs and distributed traces.

#### Interview answer

For APIs, track request rate, error rate, latency percentiles, saturation, and status codes. For dependencies, track latency, errors, timeouts, retries, circuit breaker state, and connection pool usage. For queues, track depth, age, throughput, failures, and DLQ size. Logs should include correlation IDs, and traces should show cross-service calls.

#### Deep dive

Business metrics matter too: orders created, payments authorized, duplicate attempts, failed reservations, notification delivery, and user-visible errors. Technical health without business health is incomplete.

#### Production angle

Observability should be designed before launch, not added after the first incident.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not log secrets, tokens, or full PII.
- Do not rely on logs alone when latency and dependency graphs require metrics and traces.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What are the three common observability signals?
A: Metrics, logs, and traces.

</details>

<a id="sd-review-003"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>SD-REVIEW-003. How do SLOs, SLAs and error budgets affect system design?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

They define reliability targets, customer commitments, and how much failure the system can tolerate.

#### Interview answer

An SLO is an internal reliability target, such as 99.9 percent successful requests under 300 ms. An SLA is a customer-facing commitment that may have contractual consequences. An error budget is the allowed unreliability over a period. These targets guide redundancy, monitoring, alerting, release pace, and investment.

#### Deep dive

Higher SLOs cost more and require stronger dependency management. If the error budget is being burned quickly, the team may slow releases and focus on reliability work.

#### Production angle

SLOs help avoid both underinvestment in reliability and overengineering beyond business need.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not define SLOs only around uptime if users care about latency and correctness.
- Do not promise an SLA that dependencies cannot support.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What is an error budget?
A: The amount of unreliability allowed while still meeting the SLO.

</details>

<a id="sd-review-004"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>SD-REVIEW-004. How do you explain cost trade-offs in a system design interview?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Explain which design choices increase cost and what reliability, performance, or simplicity they buy.

#### Interview answer

Cost comes from compute, storage, network, managed services, replicas, multi-region deployment, observability, development time, and operational complexity. For example, active-active multi-region may improve availability but increase infrastructure cost, data consistency complexity, and on-call burden. A cache may reduce database cost but add memory and operational work.

#### Deep dive

Cost discussion should be tied to business value. It is reasonable to start simple, measure, and evolve when usage justifies it.

#### Production angle

Senior engineers consider total cost of ownership, not only cloud bill: development, debugging, migrations, incidents, and team expertise.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not choose the most complex architecture for an unproven product.
- Do not optimize cost so aggressively that reliability or correctness suffers.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What is total cost of ownership?
A: Infrastructure plus engineering, operations, incidents, maintenance, and migration cost.

</details>

<a id="sd-review-005"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>SD-REVIEW-005. How would you design a rate limiter?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Define the limit scope, algorithm, storage, enforcement point, response behavior, and failure policy.

#### Interview answer

I would first clarify what is being limited: IP, user, API key, tenant, endpoint, or expensive operation. Then choose an algorithm such as token bucket for burst tolerance or sliding window for smoother accuracy. For one instance, in-memory counters may work; for multiple instances, Redis or a shared store with atomic operations is common.

#### Deep dive

The design must include key expiration, time source, high-cardinality memory use, distributed accuracy, observability, and response semantics. Clients should receive clear 429 responses and retry guidance when appropriate.

#### Production angle

Rate limiter failures need explicit policy: fail open, fail closed, or degrade based on endpoint risk.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not rely only on client-side rate limiting.
- Do not let limiter calls become slower than the protected operation.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What must a rate limiter define?
A: Scope, algorithm, storage, enforcement point, response behavior, and failure policy.

</details>

<a id="sd-review-006"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>SD-REVIEW-006. How would you design an order checkout flow?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Design checkout around correctness: idempotency, inventory reservation, payment state, order state, and recovery.

#### Interview answer

I would create an order with an idempotency key, validate cart and prices, reserve inventory, authorize payment, persist order state, and publish events for fulfillment and notification. The workflow can be a local transaction for one service or a Saga if inventory, payment, and fulfillment are separate services.

#### Deep dive

The order should have explicit states: pending, reserved, payment_authorized, confirmed, failed, canceled, or compensated. External calls need timeouts, retries, provider idempotency, and reconciliation for ambiguous failures.

#### Production angle

Checkout needs audit logs, duplicate prevention, stuck-order monitoring, compensation, and manual repair tooling.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not charge payment before you know how to handle inventory failure.
- Do not rely on frontend retry behavior for duplicate prevention.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What are key checkout design concerns?
A: Idempotency, inventory, payment, order state, retries, compensation, and auditability.

</details>

<a id="sd-review-007"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>SD-REVIEW-007. How would you design an async notification system?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Accept notification requests durably, enqueue delivery work, process with idempotent workers, and track delivery state.

#### Interview answer

The API or event consumer creates notification records, stores recipient/channel/template data, and publishes delivery tasks to a queue. Workers send email, SMS, push, or webhook messages through providers with rate limits, retries, and provider-specific error handling. Delivery state should be persisted for debugging and user support.

#### Deep dive

The design should handle deduplication, template versioning, user preferences, unsubscribes, provider failover, DLQs, and reprocessing. Some notifications are transactional and important; others are marketing and lower priority.

#### Production angle

Monitor accepted count, sent count, failure rate, provider latency, queue age, retry rate, DLQ size, and unsubscribe or bounce signals.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not send duplicate user-visible notifications on retry.
- Do not ignore provider rate limits and suppression lists.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What makes notification workers safe?
A: Idempotency, persistent state, rate limits, retries, DLQs, and provider-aware error handling.

</details>

<a id="sd-review-008"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>SD-REVIEW-008. How would you design a paginated feed or timeline?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Model feed reads around stable ordering, pagination cursor, freshness needs, and fan-out strategy.

#### Interview answer

I would clarify whether the feed is personalized, chronological, ranked, or filtered. For pagination, keyset or cursor-based pagination is usually better than large offsets. For scaling, choose fan-out-on-write, fan-out-on-read, or a hybrid depending on follower counts, freshness, and celebrity users.

#### Deep dive

Feed items need stable sort keys, deduplication, visibility rules, deletion handling, and cache strategy. A cursor should encode position safely without exposing fragile internal assumptions.

#### Production angle

Monitor feed generation latency, cache hit rate, fan-out backlog, duplicate item rate, and freshness lag.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not use offset pagination for deep, frequently changing feeds.
- Do not ignore privacy changes and deleted content in cached feeds.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Why prefer cursor pagination for feeds?
A: It is more stable and efficient than large offsets on changing datasets.

</details>

<a id="sd-review-009"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>SD-REVIEW-009. How would you design a file upload and processing system?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Use object storage for files, metadata in a database, direct uploads when possible, and async workers for processing.

#### Interview answer

The service can create upload metadata and return a pre-signed upload URL so the client uploads directly to object storage. After upload completion, an event or callback triggers validation and processing workers. Metadata tracks state such as pending, uploaded, processing, ready, failed, or deleted.

#### Deep dive

The design should handle file size limits, content type validation, malware scanning, retries, idempotent processing, thumbnails, access control, retention, and cleanup of abandoned uploads.

#### Production angle

Monitor upload success, processing queue age, worker failures, storage growth, scan failures, and orphaned objects.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not route large files through app servers unless required.
- Do not trust client-provided file type or size without validation.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Why use pre-signed URLs for upload?
A: To let clients upload directly to object storage without overloading app servers.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>SD-REVIEW-010. How would you design backward-compatible API evolution for mobile or external clients?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Assume clients update slowly and support old and new contracts during a migration window.

#### Interview answer

I would avoid breaking changes, add optional fields, keep old fields, use tolerant readers, and document field semantics clearly. If behavior must change, introduce a versioned endpoint or explicit capability flag. Metrics should show usage by client version so the team knows when old behavior can be removed.

#### Deep dive

Mobile and external clients may stay on old versions for months. Server deployments and database migrations must remain compatible with both old and new clients. Error formats and idempotency behavior also need stability.

#### Production angle

Backward compatibility prevents coordinated releases and reduces rollback risk.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not remove fields just because the latest app no longer uses them.
- Do not change enum meaning without a compatibility plan.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Why is mobile API compatibility hard?
A: Old app versions can remain active long after the server changes.

</details>

<a id="sd-review-011"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>SD-REVIEW-011. How would you design a booking or reservation system with limited inventory?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Protect inventory with strong consistency, reservation state, expiration, idempotency, and clear conflict handling.

#### Interview answer

I would model inventory units or capacity, create temporary reservations with expiration, and confirm them after payment or user commitment. The database should enforce constraints so two users cannot reserve the same seat or exceed capacity. Expired reservations must be released safely by a scheduled job or worker.

#### Deep dive

High-demand bookings may need queues, waitlists, optimistic locking, pessimistic locking, or partitioning by event or resource. The user experience should clearly communicate held, confirmed, expired, and failed states.

#### Production angle

Monitor reservation conflicts, expiration jobs, payment delays, oversell attempts, and stuck holds.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not rely only on cache counters for final inventory correctness.
- Do not hold inventory forever after abandoned checkout.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What protects limited inventory?
A: Database constraints, locks or optimistic versions, reservation expiry, and idempotent confirmation.

</details>

<a id="sd-review-012"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>SD-REVIEW-012. How would you evolve a simple monolith design as traffic grows?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Scale the monolith first, then split only around clear bottlenecks, ownership boundaries, or deployment needs.

#### Interview answer

I would start with a modular monolith, relational database, stateless app instances, good indexes, caching where justified, queues for slow work, and strong observability. As traffic grows, I would separate read models, workers, search, file processing, or high-change modules before splitting core services. Service extraction should follow domain boundaries and operational readiness.

#### Deep dive

Premature microservices add network failures, data consistency problems, deployment complexity, and observability requirements. A well-structured monolith can scale far and gives clearer boundaries for later extraction.

#### Production angle

Evolution should be incremental: measure bottlenecks, isolate modules, add contracts, migrate data safely, and keep rollback paths.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not split services by technical layers such as controller, service, repository.
- Do not move to microservices before the team can operate distributed systems.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What is a good first step before microservices?
A: Build a modular monolith with clear boundaries, observability, and scalable bottleneck handling.

</details>
