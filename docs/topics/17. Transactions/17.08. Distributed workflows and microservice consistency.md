# Distributed workflows and microservice consistency

---

<a id="tx-dist-001"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>TX-DIST-001. What is a distributed transaction, and why is it difficult?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

A distributed transaction tries to atomically coordinate changes across multiple transactional resources.

#### Interview answer

It is difficult because resources can fail independently, networks are unreliable, participants may be slow, and the coordinator must decide commit or rollback in the presence of partial failures.

#### Deep dive

Local transactions have one clear owner. Distributed transactions add coordination, blocking, recovery logs, timeouts, and uncertain outcomes.

#### Production angle

Many microservice systems avoid distributed transactions and instead use local transactions plus eventual consistency patterns.

#### Traps / caveats

- A chain of HTTP calls is not a distributed transaction.
- Local rollback cannot undo another service's committed state.
- Distributed consistency must be visible in API and product design.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What is a distributed transaction?
A: A transaction that coordinates atomic commit across multiple resources.

</details>

<a id="tx-dist-002"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>🧠 TX-DIST-002. What is two-phase commit, and what are its main drawbacks?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Two-phase commit asks participants to prepare, then later tells all to commit or roll back.

#### Interview answer

In phase one, the coordinator asks every participant if it can commit. If all vote yes, phase two tells them to commit. If any vote no, all roll back. The problem is blocking and operational complexity when failures happen between phases.

#### Deep dive

Participants may hold locks while prepared. Coordinator failure can leave resources waiting. Recovery is possible but adds complexity.

#### Production angle

2PC can be valid in controlled environments, but many microservices avoid it because availability and operational independence suffer.

#### Traps / caveats

- It is not a magic fix for unreliable networks.
- It can reduce service autonomy.
- Broker/database support and configuration matter.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What are the two phases of 2PC?
A: Prepare/vote, then commit or rollback decision.

</details>

<a id="tx-dist-003"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>TX-DIST-003. Why are XA transactions rarely preferred in microservice architectures?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

They tightly couple services and resources, reduce availability, and add hard operational failure modes.

#### Interview answer

Microservices usually aim for independent deployment, ownership, and failure isolation. XA coordination cuts across those boundaries and can make one service's transaction depend on another resource's availability.

#### Deep dive

XA also requires all participants to support the protocol and be configured correctly. It can be hard to debug when participants are prepared but not committed.

#### Production angle

Most teams prefer Outbox, Saga, idempotency, and reconciliation unless strong atomicity across resources is truly required.

#### Traps / caveats

- "Rarely preferred" does not mean "never valid."
- Replacing XA with eventual consistency requires product and operational design.
- Local correctness remains essential.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Why avoid XA in many microservices?
A: It couples services/resources and introduces blocking coordination failure modes.

</details>

<a id="tx-dist-004"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>TX-DIST-004. What is the Saga pattern?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

A Saga is a distributed workflow made of local transactions plus compensating actions.

#### Interview answer

Each service commits its own local transaction and publishes or triggers the next step. If a later step fails, the workflow runs compensating transactions to semantically undo or offset previous committed steps.

#### Deep dive

Saga is not database rollback. It is a business workflow pattern for eventual consistency.

#### Production angle

Use it for long-running workflows such as order, payment, inventory, shipping, or onboarding where one ACID transaction across services is unrealistic.

#### Traps / caveats

- Compensation may not restore the exact original state.
- Every step needs idempotency.
- Users need clear pending, failed, or compensated states.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What is a Saga?
A: A sequence of local transactions coordinated with compensating actions.

</details>

<a id="tx-dist-005"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>TX-DIST-005. How do choreography and orchestration differ in Saga workflows?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Choreography is event-driven coordination; orchestration uses a central workflow controller.

#### Interview answer

In choreography, services react to events and publish their own next events. In orchestration, a process manager tells services what command to execute and tracks the workflow state.

#### Deep dive

Choreography reduces central control but can become hard to trace. Orchestration improves visibility and control but adds a central component.

#### Production angle

For complex payment and inventory flows, orchestration is often easier to monitor and recover. For simple flows, choreography may be enough.

#### Traps / caveats

- Choreography can create hidden coupling.
- Orchestrators can become too smart or too central.
- Both approaches require idempotency and observability.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What is Saga orchestration?
A: A central process manager coordinates workflow steps and state.

</details>

<a id="tx-dist-006"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>TX-DIST-006. How is a compensating transaction different from rollback?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Rollback cancels uncommitted work; compensation creates a new committed action that offsets earlier committed work.

#### Interview answer

If payment was captured and inventory later fails, you cannot roll back the payment database transaction from another service. You issue a refund or reversal as a new business action.

#### Deep dive

Compensation must follow business rules and may be partial, delayed, or impossible in some domains.

#### Production angle

Design compensation explicitly with audit trails, user messaging, retry, and manual intervention paths.

#### Traps / caveats

- Compensation is not always symmetric.
- Side effects may have already reached users or external providers.
- Compensation also needs idempotency.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What is a compensating transaction?
A: A new action that semantically offsets already committed work.

</details>

<a id="tx-dist-007"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>TX-DIST-007. How would you design order creation across Order, Payment, and Inventory services?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Use local transactions per service, a Saga workflow, idempotent commands, and reliable events.

#### Interview answer

The Order service creates an order in pending state and emits an event or starts an orchestrated workflow. Payment authorizes or captures money. Inventory reserves stock. The order transitions to confirmed only when required steps complete; failures trigger compensation.

#### Deep dive

Outbox should be used for reliable event publication. Every command needs idempotency because messages and retries can duplicate.

#### Production angle

Users should see pending, confirmed, failed, or compensating states instead of assuming synchronous atomic completion.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not hold a DB transaction while calling all services.
- Handle payment success but inventory failure.
- Reconcile stuck workflows.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What pattern fits order/payment/inventory across services?
A: Saga with local transactions, outbox events, idempotency, and compensation.

</details>

<a id="tx-dist-008"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>TX-DIST-008. How can timeout ambiguity affect distributed workflow outcomes?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

A timeout means the caller stopped waiting, not necessarily that the operation failed.

#### Interview answer

If a payment call times out, the payment may have succeeded, failed, or still be processing. Retrying blindly can duplicate work unless the command is idempotent.

#### Deep dive

Distributed systems often have ambiguous outcomes. The design must support status checks, idempotency keys, deduplication, and reconciliation.

#### Production angle

Timeout ambiguity is common with payment providers, inventory reservation, shipping labels, and external APIs.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not treat every timeout as rollback.
- Use provider idempotency keys when available.
- Store request IDs and correlation IDs.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What does a timeout prove?
A: Only that the caller stopped waiting; the remote outcome may be unknown.

</details>

<a id="tx-dist-009"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>TX-DIST-009. Why should each service usually own its database?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Database ownership preserves service autonomy and clear transaction boundaries.

#### Interview answer

If multiple services share tables and transactions, they are tightly coupled at the data model and deployment level. Service-owned databases make each service responsible for its invariants and local transactions.

#### Deep dive

Shared databases can be pragmatic in a modular monolith, but in microservices they often create hidden coupling, migration conflicts, and unclear ownership.

#### Production angle

Service ownership makes scaling, schema changes, incident isolation, and access control clearer.

#### Traps / caveats

- Separate databases do not remove integration complexity.
- Cross-service queries require APIs, read models, or events.
- Do not split services before boundaries are understood.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Why should services own their databases?
A: To keep transaction boundaries, schema ownership, and deployment responsibilities clear.

</details>

<a id="tx-dist-010"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>TX-DIST-010. How should a user-facing API represent an eventually consistent workflow state?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Expose explicit states such as pending, confirmed, failed, retrying, or compensated.

#### Interview answer

If a workflow cannot complete atomically, the API should not pretend it did. Return a durable operation ID and state that clients can poll or receive updates for.

#### Deep dive

State machines make distributed workflows understandable. They help with retries, compensation, manual repair, and customer support.

#### Production angle

Clear states prevent users from seeing random errors when payment succeeded but inventory is still pending.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not return success before the business outcome is true.
- Do not hide stuck states.
- State transitions should be idempotent and auditable.

#### Optional Anki

Q: How should APIs expose eventual consistency?
A: With explicit durable workflow states.

</details>

<a id="tx-dist-011"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>TX-DIST-011. How do retries, duplicate messages, and out-of-order messages affect distributed workflows?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

They can repeat steps, apply stale transitions, or break expected workflow order.

#### Interview answer

Distributed systems commonly deliver messages at least once and sometimes out of order. Handlers must be idempotent, check current state, and reject invalid or stale transitions.

#### Deep dive

Sequence numbers, version checks, status machines, deduplication tables, and partitioning keys can help maintain correctness.

#### Production angle

Without this design, retries can double-charge, double-ship, or move an order from canceled back to confirmed.

#### Traps / caveats

- Exactly-once claims often have boundaries.
- Idempotent consumer logic is still needed.
- Ordering is usually per key, not global.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What do message handlers need under at-least-once delivery?
A: Idempotency and state validation.

</details>

<a id="tx-dist-012"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>TX-DIST-012. How would you reconcile inconsistent state between services?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Compare durable state, detect differences, and run idempotent repair or compensation.

#### Interview answer

Reconciliation means periodically or manually checking whether services agree about important business state. If not, the system decides whether to retry a missing step, compensate, or escalate for manual resolution.

#### Deep dive

Good reconciliation needs correlation IDs, event history, timestamps, workflow state, and audit logs.

#### Production angle

It is essential for payment, inventory, shipment, subscription, and accounting systems because no distributed workflow is perfect.

#### Traps / caveats

- Reconciliation should not blindly overwrite state.
- Repair actions must be idempotent.
- Some inconsistencies require human review.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What is reconciliation in distributed workflows?
A: Detecting and repairing inconsistent durable state between systems.

</details>

<a id="tx-dist-013"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>🧠 TX-DIST-013. What is a process manager, and when is it useful?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

A process manager tracks and coordinates a long-running business workflow.

#### Interview answer

It stores workflow state, sends commands to services, handles events, and decides the next step. It is useful when a Saga has many branches, retries, timeouts, and compensations.

#### Deep dive

A process manager is often the orchestration component in a Saga. It should be durable because losing workflow state can leave business processes stuck.

#### Production angle

It improves observability and supportability for complex order, payment, onboarding, or provisioning flows.

#### Traps / caveats

- It can become a centralized god service.
- It must handle duplicate and late events.
- Workflow state schema needs evolution.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What does a process manager store?
A: Durable workflow state and decisions about next steps.

</details>

<a id="tx-dist-014"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>TX-DIST-014. When would you keep a workflow in a single monolith transaction instead of splitting it into microservices?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Keep it together when the business invariant is strongly transactional and the boundaries are not mature.

#### Interview answer

If order, payment, and inventory are tightly coupled and require immediate consistency, a modular monolith may be simpler and safer. Splitting too early can replace one local transaction with distributed workflow complexity.

#### Deep dive

Microservices are an organizational and operational choice, not a default architecture upgrade.

#### Production angle

For small teams or highly coupled domains, a monolith with clear modules and local transactions can be more reliable than premature distribution.

#### Traps / caveats

- A monolith still needs good boundaries.
- A single transaction can become too large.
- Distribution may be justified by scaling, ownership, or deployment needs.

#### Optional Anki

Q: When is a monolith transaction preferable?
A: When strong consistency is required and service boundaries are not worth the distributed complexity.

</details>
