# Distributed transactions, Saga and compensating actions

---

<a id="ms-saga-001"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-SAGA-001. What is the Saga pattern, and when should you use it?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Saga coordinates a business workflow across services as a sequence of local transactions with compensating actions.

#### Interview answer

Use Saga when one business process spans multiple service-owned databases and one ACID transaction is not available or desirable. Each step commits locally, then triggers the next step. If a later step fails, compensating actions undo or neutralize earlier successful steps.

#### Deep dive

Saga manages consistency over time, not isolation like a database transaction. The domain must tolerate intermediate states.

#### Production angle

Sagas need state tracking, timeouts, retries, idempotency, monitoring, and manual repair paths.

#### Traps / caveats

- Saga is not a magic replacement for ACID.
- Some operations cannot be truly undone, only compensated.

</details>

<a id="ms-saga-002"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-SAGA-002. What is the difference between choreography and orchestration in Saga?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Choreography uses events between services; orchestration uses a central coordinator to command each step.

#### Interview answer

In choreography, each service reacts to events and publishes the next event. It is loosely coupled but can become hard to understand. In orchestration, a Saga orchestrator stores workflow state and tells participants what to do. It is more explicit but adds a central workflow component.

#### Deep dive

Choreography fits simple flows with few participants. Orchestration fits complex workflows with branching, timeouts, retries, and human visibility needs.

#### Production angle

Orchestration usually makes stuck workflow debugging easier because state is centralized.

#### Traps / caveats

- Choreography can hide the process in scattered handlers.
- Orchestrators should coordinate, not own every participant's business rules.

</details>

<a id="ms-saga-003"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-SAGA-003. How do you handle business workflows that span multiple services without one local transaction?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Use local transactions with Saga, Outbox, idempotent commands/events, retries, compensation, and reconciliation.

#### Interview answer

First decide whether the workflow really needs multiple services. If yes, each service updates its own database in a local transaction and emits a reliable event or response. A Saga coordinates progress and compensation. The system records state so ambiguous failures can be retried or repaired.

#### Deep dive

The design must distinguish technical failures, business rejections, and unknown outcomes. Each category needs different handling.

#### Production angle

Money, inventory, and fulfillment flows need audit trails and operations tooling, not only code-level retries.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not use distributed transactions by default.
- Do not ignore intermediate states visible to users or support teams.

</details>

<a id="ms-saga-004"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-SAGA-004. What are compensating transactions?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Compensating transactions are business actions that undo or offset the effect of a completed Saga step.

#### Interview answer

If inventory was reserved but payment failed, a compensation might release the reservation. If payment was captured but order creation failed, compensation might refund or void the payment. Compensation must follow business rules, not just reverse database rows.

#### Deep dive

Some effects are irreversible. Sending an email cannot be undone; you may need a correction event or follow-up notification.

#### Production angle

Compensations need monitoring and manual fallback because failed compensation can leave customers or money in inconsistent states.

#### Traps / caveats

- Compensation is not rollback with database isolation.
- Compensating actions must also be idempotent.

</details>

<a id="ms-saga-005"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-SAGA-005. Why are traditional distributed transactions / 2PC often avoided in microservices?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

They reduce availability, add blocking and operational complexity, and couple services and resources tightly.

#### Interview answer

Two-phase commit coordinates multiple resources to commit atomically, but participants can block, coordinators become critical, and not all infrastructure supports it well. In microservices, it also weakens service autonomy because participants must share transaction coordination.

#### Deep dive

2PC can be valid in narrow controlled environments, but many microservice systems prefer local transactions plus Outbox, Saga, idempotency, and reconciliation.

#### Production angle

Distributed transaction failures are hard to debug and can leave resources locked or uncertain during outages.

#### Traps / caveats

- Avoid claiming 2PC is never useful; it is often just a poor fit for autonomous services.
- Saga has its own complexity and is not automatically better for every workflow.

</details>

<a id="ms-saga-015"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-SAGA-015. How do you handle retries in a Saga?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Retry transient technical failures with backoff, deadlines, idempotency, and clear limits.

#### Interview answer

Each Saga step should classify failures. Timeouts and temporary downstream errors may be retried. Business rejections should usually move the Saga to compensation or failure. Retries need idempotent commands so a repeated step does not duplicate side effects.

#### Deep dive

Saga retries should be stateful. The workflow should know which step is pending, failed, retried, compensated, or completed.

#### Production angle

Expose retry counts, stuck states, and age of in-flight Sagas to dashboards and alerts.

#### Traps / caveats

- Infinite retries can block workflows forever.
- Retrying payment capture without idempotency can double charge.

</details>

<a id="ms-saga-016"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-SAGA-016. How do you make Saga steps idempotent?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Give every step a stable command ID or business key and store enough state to recognize repeats.

#### Interview answer

Participants should handle duplicate commands or events safely. For example, inventory reservation can use `orderId` as a unique reservation key. Payment can use an idempotency key with the provider. The participant returns the existing result for duplicate requests.

#### Deep dive

Idempotency should be enforced transactionally with the state change, often via unique constraints or command logs.

#### Production angle

Idempotent steps make retries, redelivery, failover, and manual reprocessing safe.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not rely only on in-memory deduplication.
- Idempotency keys need a retention policy aligned with retry and replay windows.

</details>

<a id="ms-saga-017"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-SAGA-017. How do you handle a failed compensating transaction?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Record it, retry if safe, alert operations, and provide a manual repair or reconciliation path.

#### Interview answer

A failed compensation is serious because the workflow is already recovering from another failure. The system should keep explicit state, retry transient failures with idempotency, and escalate when compensation cannot complete automatically.

#### Deep dive

Some compensations need human approval or external provider support. The Saga should not silently mark the workflow as done if compensation failed.

#### Production angle

Operations need dashboards showing compensation failures, affected business IDs, and available repair actions.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not drop failed compensations into logs only.
- Do not assume every business action has a perfect inverse.

</details>

<a id="ms-saga-019"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>🧠 MS-SAGA-019. How do you model Saga state?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Model it as an explicit state machine with business ID, current step, status, attempts, timestamps, and failure reason.

#### Interview answer

Saga state should show where the workflow is: pending payment, inventory reserved, compensation running, completed, failed, or needs manual repair. Store commands/events processed and correlation IDs so retries and debugging are deterministic.

#### Deep dive

An explicit state machine is easier to monitor and test than implicit state scattered across events.

#### Production angle

Support teams need to answer "what happened to this order?" without reading raw broker logs.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not infer all Saga state only from transient messages.
- State transitions must be idempotent and concurrency-safe.

</details>

<a id="ms-saga-021"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-SAGA-021. How do you monitor and debug stuck Sagas?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Track workflow state, age, step latency, retry count, correlation IDs, and dead-lettered messages.

#### Interview answer

I would inspect the Saga instance by business ID, check its current step, last event, retry history, timeout status, and participant responses. Traces and structured logs should connect the original request, commands, events, and compensations.

#### Deep dive

Stuck Sagas often come from lost events, downstream outages, poison messages, version mismatch, or non-idempotent retries.

#### Production angle

Alert on age of pending workflows, not just service errors.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not rely only on broker lag to detect stuck business workflows.
- Manual repair needs audit logging.

</details>

<a id="ms-saga-025"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-SAGA-025. How would you implement order creation with payment and inventory reservation as a Saga?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Create an order in a pending state, reserve inventory, authorize payment, then confirm or compensate.

#### Interview answer

The order service creates a pending order with an idempotency key. The Saga reserves inventory and authorizes payment using idempotent commands. If both succeed, the order is confirmed. If payment fails, release inventory. If inventory fails, void or skip payment depending on step order.

#### Deep dive

Step order matters. Authorize before capture when possible because authorization is easier to void than captured money is to refund.

#### Production angle

Use correlation IDs, state tracking, timeout handling, audit events, and manual repair for ambiguous provider outcomes.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not capture payment before you know how failed inventory will be handled.
- Do not hide pending state if completion is asynchronous.

</details>

<a id="ms-saga-026"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-SAGA-026. What are the biggest traps when using Saga for business transactions?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Assuming Saga behaves like ACID, ignoring intermediate states, weak idempotency, unclear compensation, and poor observability.

#### Interview answer

Saga does not provide isolation across services. Other users or services may observe intermediate states. Every step needs idempotency, retry policy, timeout policy, compensation rules, and clear business meaning for failed or pending states.

#### Deep dive

The domain must accept that "complete" may take time and that some failures need manual reconciliation.

#### Production angle

Most Saga pain appears in support: stuck orders, partial payment, inventory locks, and missing audit evidence.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not use Saga to paper over bad service boundaries.
- Do not forget compensating actions can fail too.

</details>

<a id="ms-saga-027"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>🧠 MS-SAGA-027. When would you use a workflow engine like Temporal or Camunda instead of a hand-rolled Saga?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Use a workflow engine when workflows are long-running, complex, stateful, and need strong visibility, retries, timers, or human steps.

#### Interview answer

For simple two- or three-step flows, hand-rolled orchestration may be enough. For workflows with branching, timeouts, compensation, human approval, many retries, and audit requirements, a workflow engine provides durable state, timers, history, and operational tooling.

#### Deep dive

The trade-off is platform dependency and learning curve versus not reinventing durable workflow mechanics.

#### Production angle

Workflow engines can reduce custom recovery code, but they become critical infrastructure that must be operated well.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not introduce a workflow engine for every simple event flow.
- Do not put all domain logic into workflow definitions without service ownership.

</details>
