# Anti-patterns and common traps

---

<a id="ms-anti-002"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-ANTI-002. What is the distributed monolith anti-pattern?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

A distributed monolith has many services but still requires tightly coordinated changes, releases, and runtime calls.

#### Interview answer

It has the cost of distributed systems without autonomy. Symptoms include shared databases, chatty synchronous calls, lockstep deployments, shared domain libraries, and unclear ownership.

#### Deep dive

Distributed monoliths often come from splitting technical layers or entities instead of business capabilities.

#### Production angle

They are hard to debug, slow to release, and prone to cascading failures.

#### Traps / caveats

- More services do not guarantee modularity.
- Network boundaries can hide tight design-time coupling.

</details>

<a id="ms-anti-003"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-ANTI-003. What is the shared database anti-pattern?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Multiple services directly read or write the same database tables as their integration mechanism.

#### Interview answer

This breaks encapsulation and couples services to schema details. It makes migrations dangerous, ownership unclear, and business rules inconsistent. APIs and events should be the integration boundary instead.

#### Deep dive

A shared database can be a temporary migration state, but it should not be the target design.

#### Production angle

Hidden SQL consumers make incidents and schema changes slow and risky.

#### Traps / caveats

- Read-only access can still create coupling.
- Separate credentials do not solve shared ownership.

</details>

<a id="ms-anti-011"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-ANTI-011. Why is synchronous chaining across many services dangerous?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

It amplifies latency, reduces availability, and makes failures cascade through the chain.

#### Interview answer

If service A calls B, B calls C, and C calls D, the user request depends on every hop. A slow or failing service can hold resources throughout the chain. Retries can multiply load across all services.

#### Deep dive

Long chains also make ownership and debugging harder because no single service sees the full user flow unless tracing is strong.

#### Production angle

Reduce chains with better boundaries, read models, async processing, and dependency isolation.

#### Traps / caveats

- Parallel calls reduce sequential latency but not dependency risk.
- Increasing timeouts hides the problem while tying up resources.

</details>

<a id="ms-anti-012"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-ANTI-012. Why is using distributed transactions everywhere a smell?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

It suggests service boundaries cut through invariants that may belong together.

#### Interview answer

If most operations need atomic updates across services, the architecture may be too fragmented. Distributed transactions also reduce availability and tie services to shared transaction coordination.

#### Deep dive

Sometimes a local transaction inside one service is the right design. Saga should be used for workflows that can tolerate intermediate states.

#### Production angle

Overuse of distributed transactions creates difficult failure recovery and operational coupling.

#### Traps / caveats

- Avoid dogma: some environments can use distributed transactions carefully.
- Do not replace every transaction with Saga without checking business semantics.

</details>

<a id="ms-anti-013"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-ANTI-013. Why is duplicating monolith modules as services often wrong?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Monolith modules may reflect old code structure, not good service boundaries.

#### Interview answer

If you lift modules into services without revisiting domain boundaries, data ownership, and workflows, you often preserve old coupling and add network failure. Extraction should be guided by business capability and ownership, not file names.

#### Deep dive

Legacy modules may have hidden dependencies and shared transactions that become painful over the network.

#### Production angle

Naive extraction creates slower flows and more incidents while delivering little autonomy.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not assume package boundaries are domain boundaries.
- Do not copy bugs and hidden coupling into new services.

</details>

<a id="ms-anti-014"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-ANTI-014. Why is 'one table = one service' usually a bad rule?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Tables are persistence details; service boundaries should follow business capabilities and invariants.

#### Interview answer

One business operation often needs several tables atomically. Splitting each table into a service creates chatty calls and distributed transactions. A service should own the data needed to enforce its business rules.

#### Deep dive

Entities can be too fine-grained for service boundaries. Aggregates or bounded contexts are better signals.

#### Production angle

Table-based services create high latency and fragile workflows in normal user paths.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not equate CRUD ownership with domain ownership.
- Database normalization is not a microservice decomposition strategy.

</details>

<a id="ms-anti-015"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-ANTI-015. Why is 'microservices first' often a bad strategy for small teams?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Small teams often get more value from a modular monolith because microservices add operational overhead before autonomy pays off.

#### Interview answer

A small team can usually coordinate inside one codebase. Microservices require CI/CD, monitoring, contract testing, security, deployment automation, and on-call maturity. Without those, delivery slows down.

#### Deep dive

The first architectural priority is clear boundaries, not separate processes.

#### Production angle

Small teams can spend more time operating infrastructure than building product.

#### Traps / caveats

- A monolith should still be modular and testable.
- Do not delay decomposition forever if real ownership or scaling constraints appear.

</details>

<a id="ms-anti-018"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-ANTI-018. What is the most common reason microservice migrations fail?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

They often fail because teams split technology before understanding domain boundaries, data ownership, and operational readiness.

#### Interview answer

The migration becomes a distributed version of the old system: shared data, unclear ownership, synchronous chains, and no reliable observability. Teams expect faster delivery but instead get more coordination and incidents.

#### Deep dive

Successful migration is incremental and domain-driven, with platform maturity growing alongside service count.

#### Production angle

Watch delivery speed, incident count, release coordination, and service ownership as migration health indicators.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not measure success by number of services created.
- Do not ignore data migration and operations until late.

</details>
