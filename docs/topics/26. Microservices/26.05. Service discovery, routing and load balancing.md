# Service discovery, routing and load balancing

---

<a id="ms-disc-001"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-DISC-001. What is Service Discovery, and why is it needed?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Service discovery lets clients find healthy instances of a service without hardcoding hostnames or IP addresses.

#### Interview answer

In dynamic environments, instances start, stop, scale, and move. Service discovery provides a stable logical name or registry lookup so callers can route requests to available instances.

#### Deep dive

Discovery can be DNS-based, registry-based, client-side, server-side, or handled by platform infrastructure such as Kubernetes Services.

#### Production angle

Bad discovery causes traffic to dead, overloaded, or unready instances, leading to timeouts and cascading failures.

#### Traps / caveats

- Service discovery is not configuration management.
- Discovery must be tied to health and readiness, not only process existence.

</details>

---

<a id="ms-disc-002"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>🧠 MS-DISC-002. What is the difference between client-side and server-side service discovery?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Client-side discovery chooses an instance in the caller; server-side discovery sends traffic through a load balancer or proxy that chooses the instance.

#### Interview answer

With client-side discovery, the client queries a registry and load-balances itself. With server-side discovery, the client calls a stable endpoint, and infrastructure routes to an instance. Client-side can be flexible but couples clients to discovery logic; server-side centralizes routing but adds infrastructure dependency.

#### Deep dive

Kubernetes Services are closer to server-side discovery from the application perspective, while older Netflix-style stacks often used client-side discovery.

#### Production angle

The choice affects rollout, retries, load-balancing policy, observability, and failure modes.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not let every service implement inconsistent discovery behavior.
- Cached instance lists need expiry and health updates.

</details>

<a id="ms-disc-009"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>🧠 MS-DISC-009. How do health checks affect service discovery?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Health checks decide whether an instance should receive traffic or be removed from routing.

#### Interview answer

Discovery should route only to instances that are ready to serve. Health signals can include process health, dependency readiness, warmup completion, and graceful shutdown state. A bad health check can remove healthy instances or keep broken ones in rotation.

#### Deep dive

Liveness and readiness should be separated. Liveness answers "should this process be restarted?" Readiness answers "should this instance receive traffic?"

#### Production angle

Health check flapping can cause traffic instability and cascading overload.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not make liveness depend on every downstream service.
- Do not mark ready before caches, migrations, or consumers are actually initialized.

</details>

<a id="ms-disc-011"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-DISC-011. How does Kubernetes Service discovery work conceptually?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Kubernetes gives a Service a stable DNS name and, for normal Services, a virtual IP that routes traffic to matching ready Pods.

#### Interview answer

Applications call a Service DNS name rather than individual Pod IPs. Kubernetes tracks endpoints for Pods selected by the Service and routes traffic to them. Readiness probes control whether a Pod is included as an endpoint.

#### Deep dive

The exact data path depends on cluster networking, but the application-level idea is stable service identity over dynamic Pod instances.

#### Production angle

Readiness, rolling updates, connection draining, and endpoint propagation delays all affect whether traffic reaches the right instances.

#### Traps / caveats

- A Pod being running does not mean it is ready.
- Headless Services are different: they expose endpoint DNS records without a ClusterIP.
- DNS caching and long-lived connections can affect rollout behavior.

</details>

<a id="ms-disc-013"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-DISC-013. What can go wrong if stale service instances remain in the registry?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Callers may route traffic to dead, draining, unready, or wrong-version instances.

#### Interview answer

Stale entries cause timeouts, connection errors, retries, and confusing partial outages. During deployments, they can send traffic to instances that are shutting down or not yet warmed up.

#### Deep dive

Stale discovery is often caused by missed deregistration, slow health propagation, bad readiness probes, or clients caching instance lists too long.

#### Production angle

It can trigger retry storms and make a small deployment issue look like broad dependency instability.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not assume registry state updates instantly.
- Graceful shutdown must coordinate with discovery removal and connection draining.

</details>

<a id="ms-disc-014"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-DISC-014. How do load balancing and service discovery work together?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Service discovery finds candidate instances; load balancing chooses where each request goes.

#### Interview answer

Discovery provides the set of available instances, and load balancing spreads traffic across them using a policy such as round-robin, least connections, locality, or weighted routing. Health and readiness signals should remove bad instances from the candidate set.

#### Deep dive

Load balancing can happen in clients, proxies, gateways, service meshes, or platform networking. The placement affects observability and control.

#### Production angle

Poor load balancing can overload one instance while others are idle, especially with long-lived connections or uneven request cost.

#### Traps / caveats

- Round-robin is not always fair if requests have very different cost.
- Retries should not repeatedly hit the same unhealthy instance.

</details>

<a id="ms-disc-019"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>🧠 MS-DISC-019. How do you prevent routing traffic to unready instances?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Use readiness checks, startup gates, graceful shutdown, connection draining, and deployment policies that wait for readiness.

#### Interview answer

An instance should not enter routing until it has loaded configuration, initialized dependencies, warmed critical caches if needed, and can serve requests. During shutdown, it should fail readiness first, stop accepting new work, finish or hand off in-flight work, then exit.

#### Deep dive

Readiness must represent application ability to serve, not just JVM process state.

#### Production angle

Routing to unready instances causes cold-start latency, failed requests, and noisy rollouts.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not use the same endpoint for liveness and readiness without thinking.
- Do not make readiness so strict that one optional downstream removes all instances.

</details>

<a id="ms-disc-020"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-DISC-020. What is the difference between readiness and liveness in service discovery contexts?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Readiness controls traffic routing; liveness controls whether the platform should restart the process.

#### Interview answer

Readiness answers "can this instance receive requests now?" Liveness answers "is this process stuck beyond recovery?" A service can be alive but not ready during startup, dependency outage, warmup, or shutdown.

#### Deep dive

Mixing the two can cause bad behavior: dependency outage may restart every Pod if liveness checks depend on downstream services.

#### Production angle

Correct probes make rolling deployments and shutdowns safer by avoiding traffic to instances that cannot serve.

#### Traps / caveats

- Liveness should not fail for temporary downstream problems in most services.
- Readiness should fail before shutdown so load balancers stop sending new traffic.

</details>
