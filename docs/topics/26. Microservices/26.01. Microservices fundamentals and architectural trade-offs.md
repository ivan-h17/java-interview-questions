# Microservices fundamentals and architectural trade-offs

---

<a id="ms-fund-001"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-FUND-001. What is a microservice?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

A microservice is an independently deployable service that owns a focused business capability and exposes it through explicit APIs or events.

#### Interview answer

A microservice is not just a small application. It should have a clear responsibility, its own lifecycle, and ideally its own data ownership boundary. Other services interact with it through contracts, not by sharing its internals.

#### Deep dive

The useful definition is operational: can this service be built, tested, deployed, scaled, and owned independently without coordinating every change with half the organization? Size matters less than autonomy, cohesion, and coupling.

#### Production angle

Microservices add network, deployment, observability, and data consistency costs, so the autonomy must be worth the operational complexity.

#### Traps / caveats

- A tiny service with shared database tables is usually not autonomous.
- A microservice should not be a remote class or CRUD wrapper around one table.

</details>

<a id="ms-fund-002"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-FUND-002. What problem does microservice architecture solve?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

It mainly solves organizational and delivery problems: independent ownership, independent deployment, and separate scaling of business capabilities.

#### Interview answer

Microservices help when one application becomes too large for teams to change safely and quickly. By splitting around business capabilities, teams can evolve services independently, release smaller changes, isolate failures better, and scale hot parts of the system separately.

#### Deep dive

The architecture trades local simplicity for distributed complexity. The gain is not "more modern code"; it is reduced coordination cost when many teams work on different parts of a large product.

#### Production angle

The benefits only appear when CI/CD, monitoring, rollback, ownership, and incident response are mature enough to operate many deployable units.

#### Traps / caveats

- Microservices do not automatically make bad domain boundaries good.
- They can slow delivery if the team is too small or infrastructure maturity is low.

</details>

<a id="ms-fund-004"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-FUND-004. What are the main drawbacks and costs of microservices?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

They add distributed systems complexity: network failures, consistency problems, observability needs, deployment coordination, and higher operational cost.

#### Interview answer

With microservices, local method calls become unreliable remote calls. Data that used to be updated in one transaction may now require Saga, Outbox, idempotency, retries, and reconciliation. You also need stronger monitoring, contract testing, service ownership, deployment automation, and security between services.

#### Deep dive

The hidden cost is coordination moving from code to runtime. If boundaries are wrong, teams end up debugging partial failures and lockstep releases instead of delivering faster.

#### Production angle

Many microservice incidents are caused by slow downstreams, retry storms, bad timeouts, schema incompatibility, missing tracing, or unclear ownership.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not adopt microservices only for fashion or resume value.
- More services mean more failure modes, not automatically more reliability.

</details>

<a id="ms-fund-005"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-FUND-005. When is a monolith a better choice than microservices?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

A monolith is better when the product, team, and domain boundaries are still changing quickly or when one team can own the whole system effectively.

#### Interview answer

For a small product or a small team, a well-structured monolith is usually simpler. It has local transactions, easier debugging, fewer deployment units, and less operational overhead. A modular monolith can preserve boundaries in code while avoiding distributed systems complexity.

#### Deep dive

Microservices are useful when the cost of coordination inside one codebase is higher than the cost of operating distributed services. Before that point, they often create accidental complexity.

#### Production angle

A monolith with clear modules, good tests, and safe deployments can scale surprisingly far and is easier to migrate later than a poorly split microservice system.

#### Traps / caveats

- "Monolith" does not have to mean unstructured code.
- Starting with microservices can freeze wrong boundaries before the domain is understood.

</details>

<a id="ms-fund-006"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-FUND-006. What is the difference between a monolith, modular monolith and microservices?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

A monolith is one deployable unit, a modular monolith has strong internal module boundaries, and microservices split those boundaries into independently deployable services.

#### Interview answer

In a monolith, all modules usually deploy together and often share one database. A modular monolith keeps one deployment but enforces clear package, API, and data boundaries inside the codebase. Microservices move selected boundaries across process and deployment boundaries.

#### Deep dive

The important distinction is not only code structure but runtime coupling. Microservices introduce remote communication, independent failures, contract evolution, and distributed data ownership.

#### Production angle

A modular monolith is often the best stepping stone because it lets teams discover boundaries before paying distributed system costs.

#### Traps / caveats

- A monolith can be modular; microservices can still be tightly coupled.
- Splitting deployment units before splitting ownership and data often creates a distributed monolith.

</details>

<a id="ms-fund-007"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-FUND-007. What do service autonomy, loose coupling and high cohesion mean in microservice architecture?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Autonomy means a service can evolve independently; loose coupling means other services rely only on stable contracts; high cohesion means the service contains related responsibilities.

#### Interview answer

A good microservice owns a business capability and can change its internal model without breaking consumers. Its API or event contracts are explicit, and its internal responsibilities belong together. If every change requires multiple services to change and deploy together, autonomy is weak.

#### Deep dive

Loose coupling has multiple forms: runtime coupling, deployment coupling, data coupling, and design-time coupling. High cohesion helps avoid both tiny "nano-services" and giant services with unrelated responsibilities.

#### Production angle

Autonomous services reduce blast radius and make on-call ownership clearer, but only if contracts, data ownership, and release practices are disciplined.

#### Traps / caveats

- Sharing a database usually destroys autonomy.
- A shared library can also create coupling if it carries domain rules used by many services.

</details>

<a id="ms-fund-008"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-FUND-008. What is independent deployability, and what operational maturity does it require?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Independent deployability means one service can be released without coordinating a simultaneous release of dependent services.

#### Interview answer

It requires backward-compatible APIs and events, contract testing, automated deployments, fast rollback or roll-forward, observability, and ownership. The service must tolerate old and new versions of consumers and producers during rolling deployment.

#### Deep dive

Independent deployability is a stronger claim than separate repositories. If releases require lockstep changes, synchronized schema updates, or manual coordination, the services are operationally coupled.

#### Production angle

Independent deployment reduces release risk only when the platform can detect bad releases quickly and route, pause, roll back, or disable behavior safely.

#### Traps / caveats

- Versioning without compatibility still causes outages.
- Feature flags do not replace contract design or migration planning.

</details>

<a id="ms-fund-013"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-FUND-013. How can microservices accidentally become a distributed monolith?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

They become a distributed monolith when services are physically separate but still tightly coupled in data, releases, runtime behavior, or ownership.

#### Interview answer

Common causes are shared databases, synchronous chains across many services, shared domain libraries, chatty APIs, and lockstep deployments. The system has all the complexity of distributed services but not the autonomy that justifies it.

#### Deep dive

Distributed monoliths often appear when a monolith is split by technical layers or tables instead of business capabilities. Services end up needing each other for every useful operation.

#### Production angle

Symptoms include cascading outages, impossible local testing, frequent multi-service incidents, and releases that require a calendar meeting.

#### Traps / caveats

- Network boundaries do not guarantee architectural boundaries.
- "One service per entity" is a frequent path to distributed monoliths.

</details>

<a id="ms-fund-015"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-FUND-015. How should team boundaries influence service boundaries?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Service boundaries should align with teams that can own, change, deploy, and operate a business capability end to end.

#### Interview answer

Microservices are partly an organizational architecture. A service should have clear ownership, on-call responsibility, and decision authority. If several teams must approve every service change, the boundary probably does not support autonomy.

#### Deep dive

Conway's Law matters: systems tend to mirror communication paths. Good service boundaries reduce cross-team coordination by giving teams full responsibility for a coherent business area.

#### Production angle

During incidents, ownership must be obvious. Ambiguous boundaries create slow triage, finger-pointing, and duplicated fixes.

#### Traps / caveats

- Team boundaries should influence architecture, but they should not override domain reality.
- Platform teams can own shared infrastructure, but domain behavior needs domain ownership.

</details>

<a id="ms-fund-017"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-FUND-017. What are the main operational requirements before adopting microservices?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

You need CI/CD, observability, automated testing, reliable deployments, incident response, service ownership, and secure service-to-service communication.

#### Interview answer

Before microservices, the organization should be able to build and deploy services automatically, monitor logs/metrics/traces, handle rollback, manage secrets, test contracts, and operate databases and queues safely. Without that, every new service increases operational risk.

#### Deep dive

The technical prerequisites matter because microservices move many problems from compile time to runtime: version skew, partial failure, latency, and cross-service debugging.

#### Production angle

If a team cannot answer "which service is slow?", "which version is deployed?", or "how do we roll back safely?", microservices will hurt.

#### Traps / caveats

- Kubernetes alone is not microservice maturity.
- Observability must be designed before incidents, not added afterward as a patch.

</details>

<a id="ms-fund-020"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-FUND-020. How would you decide whether to split a service or keep it together?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Split only when the benefit in ownership, release independence, scaling, or reliability outweighs distributed complexity.

#### Interview answer

I would look for separate business capability, clear data ownership, different change cadence, different scaling needs, different availability/security requirements, and a team ready to own it. If the parts change together, need the same transaction, or communicate constantly, keeping them together is usually better.

#### Deep dive

A split should reduce coordination, not just reduce code size. A modular monolith boundary can be a useful experiment before introducing remote communication.

#### Production angle

Bad splits create latency, duplicate data, cross-service transactions, and more incidents. Good splits reduce blast radius and release coordination.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not split because a class or table is large.
- Do not ignore operational ownership when making architectural boundaries.

</details>
