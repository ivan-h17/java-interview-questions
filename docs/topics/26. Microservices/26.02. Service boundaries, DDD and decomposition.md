# Service boundaries, DDD and decomposition

---

<a id="ms-bound-001"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-BOUND-001. How do you identify service boundaries?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Identify boundaries around business capabilities, data ownership, invariants, change cadence, and team ownership.

#### Interview answer

I start with the domain, not technical layers. I look for capabilities that have their own language, rules, lifecycle, and data. Then I check whether the candidate boundary allows a team to own and deploy the service without constant coordination.

#### Deep dive

Good boundaries minimize chatty communication and cross-service transactions. They usually align with bounded contexts, but not every bounded context must become a separate deployable service.

#### Production angle

Wrong boundaries show up as frequent cross-service incidents, slow requests with many hops, and releases that require many teams.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not split by controller/service/repository layers.
- Do not map every database table to a separate service.

</details>

<a id="ms-bound-003"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-BOUND-003. How is bounded context related to microservice boundaries?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

A bounded context is a semantic domain boundary; a microservice boundary is a deployment and ownership boundary that often follows it.

#### Interview answer

In DDD, a bounded context defines where a model and its language are valid. Microservices often use bounded contexts to avoid mixing conflicting meanings, such as "Customer" in billing versus support. But a bounded context can stay inside a modular monolith if independent deployment is not justified.

#### Deep dive

The useful rule is "service boundary should not cut through core invariants." If one business rule requires atomic changes to several concepts, they may belong together.

#### Production angle

Bounded contexts reduce accidental coupling and make API contracts more stable because services expose language that belongs to their domain.

#### Traps / caveats

- Bounded context is not the same as one entity.
- One bounded context can contain multiple services, and one service can contain several small contexts if the trade-off is intentional.

</details>

<a id="ms-bound-007"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-BOUND-007. What is domain-driven design, and why is it useful for microservices?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

DDD is an approach to modeling software around business domains, language, boundaries, and invariants.

#### Interview answer

DDD helps microservices because service boundaries should come from business behavior, not infrastructure. Concepts like bounded context, aggregate, ubiquitous language, and context mapping help teams decide what a service owns and what contracts it exposes.

#### Deep dive

DDD does not mean every project needs heavy tactical patterns. For microservices, the strategic patterns are often more important: understand subdomains, model boundaries, avoid shared models, and clarify upstream/downstream relationships.

#### Production angle

DDD-based boundaries reduce shared database pressure and make incidents easier to route to the owning domain team.

#### Traps / caveats

- DDD is not a license to over-engineer every CRUD feature.
- A DDD aggregate is not automatically a microservice.

</details>

<a id="ms-bound-008"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-BOUND-008. What is the difference between decomposition by business capability and decomposition by technical layer?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Business decomposition groups behavior by domain capability; technical decomposition splits by UI, API, service, database, or infrastructure layer.

#### Interview answer

In microservices, a business capability service owns a vertical slice: APIs, business rules, data, and integration for that capability. Technical-layer decomposition creates services like "validation service" or "database service", which forces many calls for one business operation.

#### Deep dive

Layered code can exist inside one service. The problem is making technical layers separately deployable when they cannot deliver business value independently.

#### Production angle

Business-aligned services usually have clearer ownership and fewer runtime dependencies in critical user flows.

#### Traps / caveats

- Shared infrastructure services are valid, but they should not own domain rules.
- Some platform capabilities are horizontal by nature; do not confuse those with domain services.

</details>

<a id="ms-bound-009"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-BOUND-009. Why is decomposition by technical layer usually a bad fit for microservices?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

It creates strong runtime and release coupling because one business operation must cross many services.

#### Interview answer

If persistence, validation, business rules, and API composition live in separate services, every request becomes distributed. Teams cannot release independently because a single feature touches many services. The result is often a distributed monolith.

#### Deep dive

Microservices should increase autonomy. Technical-layer services usually do the opposite because they centralize common responsibilities and force all domains through them.

#### Production angle

Layer-based decomposition increases latency, failure probability, and debugging cost in normal user paths.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not turn every shared helper into a service.
- Centralized "business rules service" often becomes a bottleneck and ownership problem.

</details>

<a id="ms-bound-012"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>🧠 MS-BOUND-012. When would you use an anti-corruption layer between services?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Use it when one service must integrate with another model without letting that external model leak into its own domain.

#### Interview answer

An anti-corruption layer translates external APIs, events, status codes, and terminology into the local model. It is useful with legacy systems, third-party APIs, or a neighboring bounded context whose language does not match yours.

#### Deep dive

The ACL can be a set of adapters, mappers, validation rules, and explicit translation objects. It protects the domain from external churn and semantic mismatch.

#### Production angle

ACLs reduce blast radius when external contracts change and make integration failures easier to classify.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not hide all errors behind generic mapping.
- Do not let the ACL become a second domain model with unclear ownership.

</details>

<a id="ms-bound-015"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-BOUND-015. How do you avoid creating chatty services?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Design boundaries and APIs so a business operation needs few coarse-grained calls, not many fine-grained remote calls.

#### Interview answer

I avoid chatty services by grouping cohesive behavior, exposing use-case-oriented APIs, using local reads or read models where appropriate, batching requests, and avoiding entity-by-entity remote lookups. If a flow needs many calls between the same services, the boundary may be wrong.

#### Deep dive

Chatty services often come from splitting by entities or technical layers. Remote calls have latency, failure, timeout, and observability costs that local method calls do not.

#### Production angle

Chatty designs amplify tail latency and make downstream incidents propagate quickly through the request path.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not expose only low-level CRUD APIs if clients need business workflows.
- Do not solve every chatty flow with caching before checking the boundary.

</details>

<a id="ms-bound-016"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-BOUND-016. How do you avoid too much duplication between services?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Avoid duplicating business authority, but allow deliberate data or code duplication when it reduces coupling.

#### Interview answer

The key is deciding which service is the source of truth. Other services may keep read models, cached copies, or translated values, but they should not independently enforce conflicting business rules for the same concept. Shared code should stay generic and stable.

#### Deep dive

Some duplication is a price of autonomy. The question is whether duplication creates inconsistency risk or whether it prevents a worse dependency.

#### Production angle

Duplicate ownership of rules causes inconsistent behavior, while deliberate read-model duplication can improve performance and resilience.

#### Traps / caveats

- DRY across service boundaries can create tight coupling.
- Duplicated data needs freshness, reconciliation, and ownership rules.

</details>

<a id="ms-bound-018"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>🧠 MS-BOUND-018. What is the difference between shared library reuse and service coupling?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Shared libraries are safe when they contain stable technical code; they create coupling when they carry domain models, rules, or release-sensitive contracts.

#### Interview answer

A logging, metrics, tracing, or HTTP utility library is usually fine. A shared `Order`, `Customer`, or business validation library is risky because many services must upgrade and reason about the same model. That reduces independent evolution.

#### Deep dive

Libraries create compile-time coupling and dependency management pressure. Services create runtime coupling. Both can be valid, but both need ownership and compatibility discipline.

#### Production angle

A breaking change in a shared domain library can force coordinated releases across many services.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not confuse avoiding duplication with preserving autonomy.
- A shared DTO library can accidentally become a hidden shared contract.

</details>

<a id="ms-bound-022"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-BOUND-022. How would you split an order management module into services?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Start by separating business capabilities such as ordering, payment, inventory, fulfillment, and notification, then validate data ownership and workflow needs.

#### Interview answer

I would first model the checkout lifecycle and identify invariants. The order service might own order state, payment service owns authorization/capture, inventory owns reservations, shipping owns fulfillment. Cross-service workflow can use Saga and events, but only if the team accepts eventual consistency.

#### Deep dive

If payment and inventory must be strongly consistent with order creation, splitting too early may be wrong. A modular monolith or fewer services may be safer until the workflow is understood.

#### Production angle

Order flows need idempotency keys, auditability, compensation, observability, and manual repair paths because failures often involve money or inventory.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not create one service per database table.
- Do not split the workflow before deciding who owns the order state machine.

</details>

<a id="ms-bound-023"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-BOUND-023. How would you detect that service boundaries are wrong in production?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Look for excessive cross-service calls, lockstep releases, duplicated ownership, unclear incidents, and data consistency problems.

#### Interview answer

Wrong boundaries show up as operational pain: a simple feature touches many services, one request fans out to many calls, teams need synchronized deployments, and incidents bounce between owners. Data repair and reconciliation may also become frequent.

#### Deep dive

Metrics can reveal this: dependency graph density, p95/p99 latency by hop, change coupling, incident ownership, and number of services touched per feature.

#### Production angle

Boundary issues are not just architecture aesthetics; they directly affect availability, latency, team speed, and incident recovery.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not judge boundaries only by code size.
- Some cross-service calls are normal; the pattern and frequency matter.

</details>

<a id="ms-bound-025"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-BOUND-025. How should service ownership and on-call responsibility influence service boundaries?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

A service should have an owner that can fix, deploy, operate, and make product decisions for that capability.

#### Interview answer

Ownership is part of the architecture. If no team can confidently own a service, its boundary is suspect. Clear ownership means the team understands the domain, monitors the service, responds to incidents, manages its data, and evolves its contracts.

#### Deep dive

Microservices without ownership become shared infrastructure nobody wants to change. That destroys the autonomy microservices are supposed to provide.

#### Production angle

During incidents, clear ownership reduces mean time to recovery because alerts, dashboards, runbooks, and escalation paths point to the right team.

#### Traps / caveats

- Avoid "shared" domain services with no accountable product owner.
- Platform ownership and domain ownership are different responsibilities.

</details>
