# Resilience patterns - timeouts, retries, Circuit Breaker, Bulkhead and Rate Limiter

---

<a id="ms-res-003"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-RES-003. How does Circuit Breaker work, and what states does it have?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

A circuit breaker stops calls to an unhealthy dependency after failures pass a threshold, then probes recovery later.

#### Interview answer

The common states are closed, open, and half-open. Closed means calls pass normally while failures are measured. Open means calls fail fast. Half-open allows limited trial calls to see whether the dependency has recovered.

#### Deep dive

Circuit breakers protect callers from wasting resources on a failing dependency and protect downstreams from overload.

#### Production angle

Monitor breaker state changes, rejected calls, downstream error rates, and recovery behavior.

#### Traps / caveats

- A circuit breaker does not fix the downstream; it limits damage.
- Thresholds based only on raw count can behave badly at low traffic.

</details>

<a id="ms-res-007"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-RES-007. What is a fallback, and when is it safe?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

A fallback is an alternative response or behavior used when the normal dependency fails.

#### Interview answer

Fallbacks are safe when degraded behavior is acceptable, such as cached recommendations, default shipping estimates, or hiding optional widgets. They are unsafe when they pretend a critical operation succeeded or violate correctness.

#### Deep dive

Good fallbacks are explicit in the product contract. The system should know whether it returned fresh, stale, partial, or default data.

#### Production angle

Track fallback rates because frequent fallback use may indicate a hidden outage.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not fallback a payment failure to "success".
- Do not hide data quality issues behind silent defaults.

</details>

---

<a id="ms-res-008"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-RES-008. When can a fallback hide a serious production problem?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

When fallback responses look successful and no one monitors how often they are used.

#### Interview answer

If a service silently serves stale cache or default values, dashboards may show low error rates while users receive wrong or degraded data. This is especially dangerous for pricing, authorization, inventory, and financial operations.

#### Deep dive

Fallback should be observable and sometimes user-visible. Not every dependency failure should be converted into HTTP 200.

#### Production angle

Alert on fallback rate, stale data age, and business correctness indicators.

#### Traps / caveats

- Availability metrics can improve while correctness gets worse.
- Fallbacks need expiry and kill-switch behavior.

</details>

<a id="ms-res-009"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-RES-009. What is the Retry pattern, and how should it be used correctly?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Retry repeats a failed operation, but only when the failure is likely transient and the operation is safe to repeat.

#### Interview answer

Use retries for temporary network errors, timeouts, rate limits, deadlocks, and similar transient failures. Combine them with backoff, jitter, max attempts, total deadline, idempotency, and error classification.

#### Deep dive

Retries are load amplifiers. A small failure can become an outage if every caller retries aggressively at the same time.

#### Production angle

Monitor retry attempts, retry success rate, and downstream load during incidents.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not retry validation or authorization failures.
- Do not retry non-idempotent operations without a deduplication strategy.

</details>

---

<a id="ms-res-012"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-RES-012. Why can retry without backoff make an outage worse?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Immediate retries increase traffic to a dependency exactly when it is least able to handle it.

#### Interview answer

If a dependency is slow or overloaded, aggressive retries multiply requests and consume more threads, connections, and CPU. Backoff and jitter spread retries over time and give the dependency a chance to recover.

#### Deep dive

Retries must fit within an overall timeout budget. Otherwise the caller waits too long and still fails.

#### Production angle

Retry storms often show up as rising traffic, rising latency, and falling success rate at the same time.

#### Traps / caveats

- More retries do not always improve reliability.
- Retrying at every layer can multiply attempts unexpectedly.

</details>

<a id="ms-res-013"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-RES-013. Which operations are safe to retry?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Read-only and idempotent operations are usually safest; non-idempotent writes need idempotency keys or deduplication.

#### Interview answer

GET-like reads are generally retryable if the system tolerates repeated load. Writes can be retried only if the server can detect duplicates and return the same result. Business failures such as invalid input or insufficient funds should not be retried.

#### Deep dive

Timeouts are ambiguous: the downstream may have completed the operation even though the caller did not see the response.

#### Production angle

Payment, order creation, and inventory reservation need explicit idempotency keys before client or service retries are allowed.

#### Traps / caveats

- HTTP method alone is not enough; implementation must be safe.
- Retrying a POST can be safe with an idempotency key and unsafe without one.

</details>

---

<a id="ms-res-014"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-RES-014. How do idempotency keys make retries safer?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

They let the receiver recognize repeated attempts for the same business operation and avoid duplicate side effects.

#### Interview answer

The client or caller sends a stable key such as order creation request ID. The receiver stores the key with the result. If the same key arrives again, it returns the original result instead of creating another order or charge.

#### Deep dive

The key must be scoped correctly, stored transactionally, and retained long enough for retries and ambiguous outcomes.

#### Production angle

Idempotency keys are essential for payment, order creation, and any write where a timeout could hide success.

#### Traps / caveats

- Random key per retry defeats the purpose.
- Reusing the same key for different operations can suppress valid work.

</details>

<a id="ms-res-016"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-RES-016. How does Bulkhead prevent cascading failures?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Bulkheads isolate resource pools so one slow dependency cannot consume all service capacity.

#### Interview answer

A service can reserve separate thread pools, connection pools, semaphores, or queues per dependency or work type. If one downstream is slow, only its allocated capacity is exhausted; other dependencies and endpoints continue to work.

#### Deep dive

Bulkhead is about containment. It accepts that some parts may fail or degrade while protecting the rest.

#### Production angle

Use bulkheads with metrics for queue depth, active calls, rejection rate, and dependency latency.

#### Traps / caveats

- Too many tiny pools can waste resources.
- A bulkhead without timeout can still fill and stay blocked.

</details>

<a id="ms-res-021"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-RES-021. What is load shedding?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Load shedding rejects or drops lower-priority work to protect critical capacity.

#### Interview answer

When a service is overloaded, accepting all work can make everyone fail slowly. Load shedding fails fast for optional, low-priority, or excessive requests so the system can preserve critical flows.

#### Deep dive

It can be implemented through rate limits, queue limits, adaptive concurrency, priority queues, or explicit overload responses.

#### Production angle

Load shedding should return clear errors such as 429 or 503 and expose metrics so clients can back off.

#### Traps / caveats

- Shedding critical writes may be unacceptable without product agreement.
- Silent dropping is dangerous unless the workflow explicitly supports it.

</details>

<a id="ms-res-023"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-RES-023. What is a timeout budget?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

A timeout budget is the total time allowed for a request or workflow, divided across operations inside it.

#### Interview answer

If the user-facing API has a 700 ms target, each downstream call must fit inside that budget. The caller should pass deadlines or enforce remaining time instead of letting each dependency use an independent large timeout.

#### Deep dive

Timeout budgets prevent hidden latency amplification from nested calls and retries.

#### Production angle

Budgeting makes p95/p99 latency predictable and helps decide which dependencies are optional.

#### Traps / caveats

- Per-call timeouts that exceed the user deadline are useless.
- Retries must also consume the same budget.

</details>

---

<a id="ms-res-024"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-RES-024. How do you choose timeout values for chained service calls?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Start from the end-to-end deadline, subtract known overhead, and allocate realistic budgets to each dependency.

#### Interview answer

Use production latency percentiles, business priority, and dependency criticality. Optional calls should have shorter budgets and fallback behavior. Critical calls need enough time to succeed normally but not so much that they exhaust the caller's request budget.

#### Deep dive

For parallel fan-out, the slowest required dependency controls total latency. For sequential calls, budgets add up.

#### Production angle

Review timeout metrics after deployment; values should be tuned from real behavior, not only estimates.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not copy one global timeout to every dependency.
- Do not set retries that exceed the remaining budget.

</details>

<a id="ms-res-026"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-RES-026. How do you prevent cascading failures?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Use timeouts, bounded retries, bulkheads, circuit breakers, backpressure, load shedding, and graceful degradation.

#### Interview answer

The core idea is containment. A dependency failure should not exhaust all threads, connections, queues, or CPU in its callers. Set limits, fail fast when appropriate, degrade optional features, and keep observability focused on dependency health.

#### Deep dive

Cascading failures often start with latency, not hard errors. Slow dependencies are especially dangerous because they tie up resources.

#### Production angle

Dashboards should show dependency latency, saturation, retry volume, open breakers, and rejected work.

#### Traps / caveats

- Blind retries are a common cause of cascades.
- Scaling the caller can make the downstream fail faster.

</details>

<a id="ms-res-028"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-RES-028. How does backpressure relate to resilience in microservice systems?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Backpressure keeps overload from becoming unbounded resource growth and cascading failure.

#### Interview answer

When a downstream is slow, upstream services should slow down, reject, queue within limits, or pause consumption instead of accumulating unlimited work. Backpressure makes capacity constraints visible and controlled.

#### Deep dive

Backpressure is different from retry. Retry adds work; backpressure limits or delays work.

#### Production angle

Use bounded queues, consumer pause/resume, concurrency limits, rate limits, and overload responses with metrics.

#### Traps / caveats

- Unbounded async queues can hide overload until memory fails.
- More consumers are not helpful if the downstream bottleneck is fixed.

</details>

<a id="ms-res-031"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>MS-RES-031. In which order would you apply timeout, retry and circuit breaker?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Use an overall timeout/deadline, retry only safe transient failures within that budget, and let the circuit breaker stop calls to unhealthy dependencies.

#### Interview answer

The exact library order can vary, but conceptually every attempt needs a timeout, retries need max attempts/backoff/jitter, and the circuit breaker should observe failures and reject when the dependency is unhealthy. Bulkheads should limit concurrent calls around the dependency.

#### Deep dive

A common failure is nested policies that multiply time or attempts beyond the caller's budget.

#### Production angle

Expose metrics per policy: attempts, timeouts, breaker opens, bulkhead rejections, and final outcomes.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not retry calls rejected by an open breaker.
- Do not place retries at multiple layers without calculating total attempts.

</details>
