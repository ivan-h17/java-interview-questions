# Producers, partitioning, batching and compression

---

<a id="kf-prod-001"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>KF-PROD-001. How does a Kafka producer send records to Kafka?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

The producer fetches cluster metadata, serializes records, chooses a partition, batches records, and sends them to the partition leader broker.

#### Interview answer

A producer is configured with bootstrap servers. It connects, discovers topic and partition metadata, serializes key/value data, chooses a partition, and sends records to the leader for that partition. The producer client batches records and handles acknowledgments, retries and errors.

#### Deep dive

The bootstrap servers are only entry points. After metadata discovery, the producer knows which broker leads each partition. Delivery behavior depends on settings such as `acks`, idempotence, batching, timeouts and retries.

#### Production angle

Producer failures should be visible and bounded. A Java service must decide whether a failed publish fails the request, goes to Outbox, is retried, or triggers degraded behavior.

#### Traps / caveats

- A send being accepted by the client buffer is not the same as being durably written.
- Async sends still need failure handling.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Where does a producer send a partition record?
A: To the broker that is leader for that partition.

</details>

<a id="kf-prod-002"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>KF-PROD-002. How do `acks` and `min.insync.replicas` affect producer durability and latency?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

`acks` controls how many broker acknowledgments the producer waits for; `min.insync.replicas` defines how many in-sync replicas are required for successful writes with strong durability.

#### Interview answer

With `acks=0`, the producer does not wait for broker confirmation. With `acks=1`, the leader acknowledges after writing. With `acks=all`, the leader waits for enough in-sync replicas. `min.insync.replicas` makes `acks=all` meaningful by requiring a minimum number of replicas to accept writes.

#### Deep dive

Higher durability usually increases latency and can reduce availability during replica problems. If ISR falls below the minimum, writes fail instead of silently accepting data with insufficient replication.

#### Production angle

For critical business events, `acks=all` plus appropriate replication factor and `min.insync.replicas` is common. The service must handle write failures as a correctness event, not ignore them.

#### Traps / caveats

- `acks=all` alone is not enough if `min.insync.replicas` is too low.
- Stronger durability can produce more visible failures during broker incidents.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What setting pairs with `acks=all` for durability?
A: `min.insync.replicas`.

</details>

<a id="kf-prod-003"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>KF-PROD-003. How do batching, `batch.size`, `linger.ms` and compression affect throughput and latency?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Batching and compression improve throughput by sending fewer, larger requests, but waiting to fill batches can increase latency.

#### Interview answer

`batch.size` controls the target batch buffer per partition, and `linger.ms` allows the producer to wait briefly for more records before sending. Larger batches and compression reduce request overhead, network bytes and broker work. The trade-off is extra wait time and memory.

#### Deep dive

Compression is usually applied to batches, so larger batches often compress better. For high-throughput systems, a small latency increase can dramatically improve efficiency. For low-latency events, large linger values may be unacceptable.

#### Production angle

Tune with real payloads and SLOs. Watch producer latency, batch size, compression ratio, request rate, buffer pressure and broker throughput.

#### Traps / caveats

- Bigger batches do not help if traffic per partition is low.
- Compression can move the bottleneck from network to CPU.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What does `linger.ms` trade?
A: It trades a small wait for better batching and throughput.

</details>

<a id="kf-prod-004"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>KF-PROD-004. What are the practical trade-offs between common Kafka compression codecs?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Choose based on CPU cost, compression ratio, latency and compatibility; `lz4` and `zstd` are common practical choices.

#### Interview answer

`gzip` often compresses well but costs more CPU and latency. `snappy` is fast but usually compresses less. `lz4` is fast with good latency. `zstd` often gives strong compression with good performance, but the exact result depends on payload and client/broker support.

#### Deep dive

Compression reduces network and disk usage and can improve throughput. It is especially useful for verbose JSON. But producers and consumers pay CPU to compress/decompress, so benchmark with real message sizes.

#### Production angle

Monitor CPU, request latency, network bytes and broker disk usage before and after changing compression.

#### Traps / caveats

- The best codec depends on data shape.
- Compression can hide large-payload design problems.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Why benchmark Kafka compression?
A: Codec trade-offs depend on payload, CPU and latency requirements.

</details>

<a id="kf-prod-005"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>KF-PROD-005. How do producer retries, timeouts and retry backoff interact?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Retries reattempt transient sends until delivery succeeds or the overall delivery timeout is reached, with backoff between attempts.

#### Interview answer

Producer retries help with transient broker or network failures. Request timeout controls how long a request can wait for a response, retry backoff controls the pause before retrying, and delivery timeout bounds the total time a record may spend trying to be delivered.

#### Deep dive

Retries can improve availability but may increase latency and duplicate risk if idempotence is not enabled. Timeout values must match the application's request latency budget and failure policy.

#### Production angle

For request-path publishing, unbounded waiting can exhaust threads. For critical events, Outbox may be better than depending only on in-memory producer retries.

#### Traps / caveats

- Retrying after an unknown result can create duplicates without idempotence.
- Too-short timeouts can fail during normal broker leadership changes.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What bounds total producer retry time?
A: `delivery.timeout.ms`.

</details>

<a id="kf-prod-006"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>🧠 KF-PROD-006. How does an idempotent producer prevent duplicate writes caused by retries?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Kafka assigns producer identity and sequence numbers so the broker can detect duplicate sends for a producer-partition stream.

#### Interview answer

With idempotence enabled, the producer attaches metadata that lets brokers recognize whether a retried batch has already been appended. If the producer times out and retries the same batch, Kafka can avoid appending it twice for that producer session and partition.

#### Deep dive

Idempotence improves producer-to-broker semantics, but it is not an end-to-end business idempotency solution. It does not prevent duplicate events from application bugs, restarted workflows, manual replay or multiple producers creating the same business event.

#### Production angle

Use producer idempotence for important writes, but still design consumers to be idempotent. Business correctness should not rely only on producer configuration.

#### Traps / caveats

- Idempotent producer does not deduplicate external side effects.
- It does not replace event IDs or consumer idempotency.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What does producer idempotence protect against?
A: Duplicate broker appends from producer retries in its supported scope.

</details>

<a id="kf-prod-007"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>🧠 KF-PROD-007. How can `max.in.flight.requests.per.connection` affect ordering?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Multiple in-flight requests can allow a later batch to succeed before an earlier retried batch, which can affect ordering if not safely configured.

#### Interview answer

This setting limits how many unacknowledged requests a producer can have per broker connection. Higher values improve throughput. Historically, if retries were enabled and idempotence was not used safely, a failed earlier batch could be retried after a later batch succeeds, causing reordering.

#### Deep dive

Modern producer idempotence reduces this risk under supported settings, but the concept remains important: throughput settings can change delivery ordering behavior. For strict ordering, use safe producer settings and a consistent partition key.

#### Production angle

For ledger, payment or lifecycle topics, review producer settings as part of correctness, not only performance.

#### Traps / caveats

- Partition ordering depends on producer behavior as well as broker log order.
- Higher throughput settings may not be acceptable for strict ordering workloads.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What can high in-flight request count trade away?
A: Simpler ordering behavior under retries if not safely configured.

</details>

<a id="kf-prod-008"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>KF-PROD-008. What is producer backpressure, and what happens when the producer buffer is full?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Producer backpressure happens when records are produced faster than they can be sent; once the buffer is full, sends block or fail after waiting.

#### Interview answer

The producer stores unsent records in memory. If brokers are slow, the network is saturated, metadata is unavailable, or the application produces too fast, the buffer fills. Calls to send may block up to a configured maximum wait and then throw an exception.

#### Deep dive

Backpressure is a signal that the application is exceeding the producer or cluster's ability to drain data. Ignoring it can increase latency, memory pressure and request thread exhaustion.

#### Production angle

A service should monitor producer buffer metrics and decide whether to slow intake, shed load, write to Outbox, or fail fast.

#### Traps / caveats

- Increasing buffer memory only delays the problem if throughput remains insufficient.
- Blocking producer sends on request threads can cascade into an outage.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What does a full producer buffer indicate?
A: The producer cannot send records as fast as the application supplies them.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>KF-PROD-009. How would you tune a Kafka producer for high throughput?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Use batching, compression, enough partitions, appropriate acknowledgments, and avoid per-record synchronous waits.

#### Interview answer

I would increase batching with `batch.size` and modest `linger.ms`, enable a suitable compression codec, send asynchronously, ensure enough partitions for parallelism, and size buffers for expected bursts. I would monitor request rate, batch size, compression ratio, latency, errors and broker load.

#### Deep dive

Throughput tuning is end-to-end. The producer, network, broker disk, replication settings and consumer capacity all matter. Stronger durability settings may reduce throughput, so the target depends on business criticality.

#### Production angle

Tune against real payloads and failure scenarios. A setting that performs well in a benchmark may behave badly during broker failover.

#### Traps / caveats

- Synchronous `send().get()` per message destroys throughput.
- High throughput with no consumer capacity just creates lag.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Main producer throughput tools?
A: Batching, compression, async sends and enough partitions.

</details>

<a id="kf-prod-010"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>KF-PROD-010. How would you tune a Kafka producer for low latency?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Reduce artificial batching delay, keep payloads small, avoid overloaded brokers, and use settings that meet the durability/latency trade-off.

#### Interview answer

I would keep `linger.ms` low, avoid huge batches for sparse traffic, use a fast compression codec or no compression depending on payload, keep messages small, and monitor request latency. I would also check DNS, TLS, broker load, partition leadership and retries.

#### Deep dive

Low latency does not always mean `acks=0`. Critical systems may accept a few milliseconds for stronger durability. The real goal is predictable latency within the business SLO.

#### Production angle

Track p95/p99 producer latency, retry rate, throttling, metadata refreshes and broker-side request queue time.

#### Traps / caveats

- Disabling durability to reduce latency can lose business events.
- Very small batches may increase CPU and network overhead.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What producer setting commonly adds batching delay?
A: `linger.ms`.

</details>

<a id="kf-prod-011"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>KF-PROD-011. How should a Java service handle producer send failures?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Handle them explicitly according to business criticality: retry, fail the request, persist to Outbox, degrade, or alert.

#### Interview answer

For critical events, I avoid fire-and-forget. The service should observe send callbacks or futures, classify errors, and decide what happens if Kafka is unavailable. Often the safest design is Transactional Outbox: commit business state and an outbox row in one database transaction, then publish asynchronously.

#### Deep dive

Producer failures can mean timeout, authorization error, serialization failure, unknown topic, record too large, buffer exhaustion or broker unavailability. Some are retryable; some require code or configuration changes.

#### Production angle

Failures need metrics, logs with correlation IDs, alerting and a runbook. Silent publish loss is usually worse than a visible failure.

#### Traps / caveats

- Ignoring async send callbacks loses errors.
- Retrying non-retryable serialization or authorization failures just creates noise.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Best pattern for reliable DB change plus Kafka publish?
A: Transactional Outbox.

</details>
