# Rebalancing and consumer group protocols

---

<a id="kf-reb-001"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>KF-REB-001. What is consumer group rebalancing, and when does it happen?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Rebalancing is the process of redistributing partitions among consumers in a group.

#### Interview answer

A rebalance happens when consumers join or leave, crash, exceed polling/heartbeat limits, change subscription, or when topic partitions change. Kafka revokes and assigns partitions so every partition has an owner in the group.

#### Deep dive

Rebalancing is necessary for elasticity and failover, but it can interrupt processing. During a rebalance, consumers may stop fetching, revoke partitions, commit offsets and receive new assignments.

#### Production angle

Frequent rebalances cause lag, duplicate processing and noisy incidents. They often point to unstable deployments, slow processing or bad timeout settings.

#### Traps / caveats

- Rebalance is normal occasionally; a storm is not.
- Long handlers can look like crashed consumers to the group.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What does Kafka rebalance redistribute?
A: Topic partitions among consumers in a group.

</details>

<a id="kf-reb-002"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>🧠 KF-REB-002. Why can rebalancing be expensive for a backend service?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Rebalancing can pause consumption, move partition ownership, force state reloads and increase duplicate processing risk.

#### Interview answer

When partitions are revoked, a consumer may need to finish in-flight work, commit offsets and release local resources. New owners may need to warm caches or rebuild local state. If this happens frequently, throughput drops and lag grows.

#### Deep dive

The cost depends on the consumer. Stateless simple consumers may recover quickly. Stateful consumers, batch processors, or consumers with external locks and local caches can suffer much more.

#### Production angle

Rebalance frequency should be monitored. A deploy strategy that restarts all consumers at once can create avoidable lag spikes.

#### Traps / caveats

- Rebalances can cause duplicates if old work finishes after ownership moves.
- Bigger consumer groups are not always more stable.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Why are frequent rebalances bad?
A: They interrupt processing and can increase lag and duplicates.

</details>

<a id="kf-reb-003"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>🧠 KF-REB-003. How do eager assignment, cooperative rebalancing and static membership differ?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Eager rebalancing revokes broadly, cooperative rebalancing moves partitions incrementally, and static membership reduces churn for restarted consumers.

#### Interview answer

In eager rebalancing, consumers typically revoke their partitions before reassignment, causing more disruption. Cooperative rebalancing lets consumers keep partitions that do not need to move. Static membership gives instances stable identities so short restarts do not always force full redistribution.

#### Deep dive

These features reduce partition movement and pause time, especially for stateful or high-throughput consumers. They do not fix slow handlers or broken deploy practices by themselves.

#### Production angle

For Spring Kafka services with frequent rolling deploys, cooperative assignment and stable instance IDs can reduce lag spikes when configured correctly.

#### Traps / caveats

- Static membership requires stable unique instance identities.
- Cooperative rebalancing still needs correct revocation handling.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What does cooperative rebalancing reduce?
A: Unnecessary partition revocation and movement.

</details>

<a id="kf-reb-004"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>KF-REB-004. How do long processing times and slow polling trigger rebalance storms?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

If processing blocks polling too long, Kafka considers the consumer unhealthy and reassigns its partitions.

#### Interview answer

A consumer must continue polling within `max.poll.interval.ms`. If one batch takes too long, the consumer may be removed from the group. Its partitions move to another consumer, which may also struggle, causing repeated rebalances and growing lag.

#### Deep dive

Common causes include large batches, slow database writes, blocked external APIs, synchronous retries, deadlocks and insufficient thread pools. The consumer is alive, but from the group perspective it is not making poll progress.

#### Production angle

Mitigate with smaller batches, bounded retries, pause/resume, async handoff with careful offset management, downstream rate limiting and realistic poll interval settings.

#### Traps / caveats

- Increasing `max.poll.interval.ms` may only delay detection.
- Handing work to another thread does not remove the need for safe commits.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Which setting can remove a slow-polling consumer from the group?
A: `max.poll.interval.ms`.

</details>

<a id="kf-reb-005"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>🧠 KF-REB-005. How should a consumer handle partition revocation and assignment?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

On revocation, finish or stop work safely and commit progress; on assignment, initialize state and resume from the correct offsets.

#### Interview answer

With a rebalance listener, the consumer can react when partitions are revoked or assigned. Before losing partitions, it should commit offsets for successfully processed records, stop accepting new work for those partitions, and clean up local state. On assignment, it may load state, seek offsets if needed, and resume processing.

#### Deep dive

This is especially important for manual commits, local state stores, external locks, file handles and partition-specific caches. The goal is to avoid duplicate side effects and stale ownership assumptions.

#### Production angle

Graceful shutdown should follow the same idea: stop polling new work, complete bounded in-flight work, commit and leave the group cleanly.

#### Traps / caveats

- Committing offsets for unfinished work can lose messages.
- Continuing to process revoked partitions can create duplicates.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What should happen before partition revocation completes?
A: Commit only successfully processed progress and stop work for revoked partitions.

</details>

<a id="kf-reb-006"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>KF-REB-006. How can rebalancing create duplicate processing?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

If a consumer processes records but does not commit before losing partitions, the new owner may process the same records again.

#### Interview answer

During rebalance, partitions move. If old processing succeeded but offsets were not committed, the new consumer resumes from the last committed offset and repeats the records. If old processing continues after revocation, both old and new owners may perform side effects.

#### Deep dive

This is a normal consequence of at-least-once processing. It becomes a bug only when handlers are not idempotent or revocation is handled unsafely.

#### Production angle

Use idempotency keys, database unique constraints and bounded shutdown. Track duplicates as expected operational events, not impossible anomalies.

#### Traps / caveats

- A rebalance can expose non-idempotent side effects.
- Duplicate processing does not always mean Kafka delivered two records.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Why can rebalance cause duplicates?
A: Ownership changes before processed offsets are committed.

</details>

<a id="kf-reb-007"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>KF-REB-007. How would you troubleshoot frequent rebalances in production?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Check consumer restarts, poll interval violations, heartbeat/session timeouts, deployment behavior, slow processing and group membership changes.

#### Interview answer

I would inspect consumer logs for rebalance reasons, `max.poll.interval.ms` violations and disconnects. Then I would check deployment events, crash loops, GC pauses, CPU saturation, network issues, batch processing time, downstream latency and partition assignment strategy.

#### Deep dive

Rebalance storms usually have a trigger: slow handlers, too-large batches, bad liveness probes, rolling deploys that restart too many instances, unstable networking, or clients exceeding timeouts.

#### Production angle

Correlate rebalance metrics with lag, error rate, pod restarts, GC pauses and database/API latency. Fix the cause, not just the timeout.

#### Traps / caveats

- Raising timeouts can reduce symptoms while increasing failure detection time.
- Restarting all consumers can make the storm worse.

#### Optional Anki

Q: First things to check for rebalance storms?
A: Restarts, poll time, heartbeats, slow dependencies and deploy events.

</details>
