# Monitoring, observability and operations

---

<a id="kf-ops-001"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>KF-OPS-001. What Kafka broker, producer and consumer metrics matter most for Java backend systems?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Watch broker health, producer errors/latency, consumer lag/processing and downstream effect metrics.

#### Interview answer

For brokers: under-replicated partitions, offline partitions, request latency, disk, network and controller health. For producers: send rate, error rate, retry rate, request latency and buffer pressure. For consumers: lag, processing time, poll behavior, commit failures, rebalances, DLQ and retry counts.

#### Deep dive

Kafka metrics must be tied to business flow metrics. A consumer can read messages while failing to update the database.

#### Production angle

Every critical pipeline needs dashboards by topic, consumer group, partition and service version.

#### Traps / caveats

- Cluster metrics alone do not prove application correctness.
- Aggregate lag can hide one broken partition.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What are three important consumer metrics?
A: Lag, processing time and error/DLQ rate.

</details>

<a id="kf-ops-002"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>KF-OPS-002. Why is consumer lag not always enough, and when should time lag be measured?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Offset lag counts records, but time lag shows business delay.

#### Interview answer

Offset lag does not account for message size, processing cost or event age. A lag of 1,000 small events may be harmless, while a lag of 10 payment events older than 30 minutes may be critical. Time lag should be measured when freshness matters.

#### Deep dive

Time lag can be measured from event timestamp, broker timestamp or business timestamp, depending on what the SLO means.

#### Production angle

Alert on "oldest unprocessed event age" for critical flows like payment, fulfillment and notifications.

#### Traps / caveats

- Producer clock skew can distort event-time lag.
- Lag of zero does not mean all side effects completed.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Why measure time lag?
A: It maps Kafka backlog to business delay.

</details>

<a id="kf-ops-003"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>KF-OPS-003. How do you measure end-to-end event latency and processing time?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Carry timestamps and correlation IDs, then measure publish time, consume time and processing completion time.

#### Interview answer

I would record when the event was created, when it was produced to Kafka, when the consumer received it, and when the business action finished. Processing time is consume-to-complete. End-to-end latency is event-created or produced-to-complete, depending on the SLO.

#### Deep dive

For asynchronous flows, traces may span producer and consumer through headers. Metrics should preserve topic, consumer group and outcome labels without excessive cardinality.

#### Production angle

Use histograms for p95/p99 latency and correlate spikes with lag, retries and downstream dependency latency.

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not use only log timestamps from different machines without considering clock skew.
- Avoid high-cardinality labels like raw order IDs in metrics.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What is processing time?
A: Time from consumer receiving a record to finishing its business work.

</details>

<a id="kf-ops-004"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>🧠 KF-OPS-004. Which replication and partition-health metrics should be monitored?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Monitor under-replicated partitions, under-min-ISR partitions, offline partitions, ISR shrink/expand and leader imbalance.

#### Interview answer

Under-replicated partitions indicate replica lag or failure. Under-min-ISR partitions threaten producer writes with strong durability. Offline partitions are unavailable. ISR churn and leader imbalance can reveal broker instability or overloaded nodes.

#### Deep dive

These are often platform-level metrics, but application teams feel them as send failures, timeouts and consumption gaps.

#### Production angle

Critical topics should alert quickly when partitions are offline or below minimum ISR.

#### Traps / caveats

- A topic can be writable but less redundant than expected.
- Ignoring ISR shrink can turn the next broker failure into data loss or unavailability.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Which metric means partitions are unavailable?
A: Offline partitions.

</details>

<a id="kf-ops-005"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>🧠 KF-OPS-005. Which broker disk, network and JVM metrics help during Kafka incidents?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Check disk usage, disk I/O, network throughput, request queues, heap/GC, CPU and page cache pressure.

#### Interview answer

Kafka incidents often involve storage or network saturation. Disk full can stop brokers. Slow disk affects request latency and replication. Network saturation affects producers, consumers and followers. JVM metrics such as GC pauses can explain broker stalls.

#### Deep dive

Kafka relies heavily on the OS page cache, so memory pressure can hurt read performance. Broker JVM heap is not the only memory story.

#### Production angle

During incidents, correlate broker metrics with topic traffic, partition movement, large messages and consumer catch-up behavior.

#### Traps / caveats

- Broker disk full is urgent because retention cleanup may not recover fast enough.
- JVM heap metrics alone do not show page cache health.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Why monitor broker disk usage?
A: Kafka stores retained logs on disk and can fail badly when disks fill.

</details>

<a id="kf-ops-006"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>KF-OPS-006. Which client metrics matter in Java services?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Producer latency/errors/retries/buffer usage and consumer lag/processing/rebalances/commit errors matter most.

#### Interview answer

For producers, monitor record send rate, error rate, retry rate, request latency, batch size and buffer exhaustion. For consumers, monitor records consumed, processing duration, poll interval, commit latency/failures, rebalances and lag.

#### Deep dive

Add business outcome metrics: records successfully applied, skipped duplicates, sent to DLQ, failed validation and retried.

#### Production angle

Client metrics should include service version and deployment correlation so a bad release is visible quickly.

#### Traps / caveats

- Kafka client success does not mean business success.
- Too many high-cardinality tags can break metrics systems.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Name one producer backpressure metric area.
A: Buffer usage/exhaustion.

</details>

<a id="kf-ops-007"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>KF-OPS-007. How do you monitor rebalances, DLQ rate, retry backlog and schema compatibility failures?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Expose separate counters and gauges for each failure path, and alert on sustained abnormal behavior.

#### Interview answer

Rebalance count and duration show group instability. DLQ rate shows records leaving the normal path. Retry backlog shows delayed work. Schema compatibility or deserialization failures show producer/consumer contract problems. These should be visible per topic and consumer group.

#### Deep dive

A healthy main consumer lag can hide a retry-topic backlog or DLQ growth. Monitoring must include the whole processing topology.

#### Production angle

Alert thresholds should reflect business impact: error percentage, oldest retry age, DLQ growth rate and affected topic criticality.

#### Traps / caveats

- DLQ count without owner or age is not actionable.
- Retry backlog can become a hidden queue outage.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Why monitor retry backlog separately?
A: Main topic lag may look fine while delayed work accumulates.

</details>

<a id="kf-ops-008"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>KF-OPS-008. How do correlation IDs and distributed traces work with asynchronous Kafka flows?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Propagate correlation or trace context in Kafka headers so producer and consumer work can be linked.

#### Interview answer

The producer puts trace context, correlation ID or causation ID into headers. The consumer extracts it and starts or continues a trace span for processing. This connects the original request, Kafka publish and asynchronous handling.

#### Deep dive

Asynchronous traces are not a single call stack. You often model producer send and consumer processing as related spans with messaging metadata such as topic, partition and offset.

#### Production angle

Tracing helps answer "which request created this event" and "which consumer failed to process it."

#### Traps / caveats

- Do not create high-cardinality metrics from correlation IDs.
- Make sure retry and DLQ publication preserve useful trace context.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Where is trace context usually stored in Kafka records?
A: Headers.

</details>

<a id="kf-ops-009"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>KF-OPS-009. What should alerts on Kafka-based flows actually mean?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

Alerts should map to user or business risk, not just raw Kafka counters.

#### Interview answer

A useful alert says something like "payment events older than 10 minutes are unprocessed" or "order event DLQ is growing after deployment." Raw lag thresholds are less useful without topic criticality, event age, processing rate and error context.

#### Deep dive

Kafka creates many possible metrics. Alert fatigue happens if every transient rebalance or small lag spike pages someone.

#### Production angle

Define SLOs per flow: maximum event age, acceptable DLQ rate, replay recovery time and producer publish success rate.

#### Traps / caveats

- Alerting only on broker health misses consumer business failures.
- Alerting only on total lag hides hot partitions.

#### Optional Anki

Q: What should Kafka alerts be tied to?
A: Business impact and recovery action.

</details>

<a id="kf-ops-010"></a>

<details>
<summary><strong>🧠 KF-OPS-010. What operational tasks should a Java backend engineer understand even when Kafka is managed by a platform team?</strong></summary>

#### Short answer

They should understand topic creation requirements, retention, ACLs, schema rollout, lag triage, replay, offset reset and incident communication.

#### Interview answer

Even with managed Kafka, application teams own how they use it. A backend engineer should know how to request topics, choose keys, set retention, configure clients, interpret lag, handle DLQ, roll out schema changes, and coordinate safe replays or offset resets with the platform team.

#### Deep dive

Platform teams keep Kafka running; service teams keep business pipelines correct. The boundary must be clear.

#### Production angle

Runbooks should say who can reset offsets, who owns DLQ records, who approves retention changes and how to pause consumers safely.

#### Traps / caveats

- "Managed Kafka" does not mean "managed event correctness."
- Offset reset is an application-risk operation, not only an admin command.

#### Optional Anki

Q: Who owns event-processing correctness?
A: The application/service team.

</details>
